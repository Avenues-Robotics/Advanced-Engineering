### Overview
In Project 1, you wrote requirements for your subsystem, including performance requirements with numbers attached. In Project 3, you checked your CAD model against those requirements before you built anything. Now the subsystem exists. In Project 4, you find out whether it actually does what your requirements say it must, and then you make it better, one documented change at a time.
This project has two halves. First you **verify**: run the tests your MOPs describe and make an honest pass, fail, or inconclusive judgment for each requirement. Then you **iterate**: choose a requirement your subsystem doesn't meet, and close the gap with evidence instead of guesswork. The iteration half is where most of the learning happens, and it is where most of your grade comes from.
<callout icon="🔁" color="gray_bg">
	**Iteration is a process, not an outcome**
	You are assessed on how well you iterate, not on whether your subsystem ends up passing. A well-documented sequence of changes that ends with the subsystem still failing, or with the team cutting it from the robot, can still show advanced understanding of the iteration process. On the other hand, a subsystem that passes after a series of undocumented tweaks cannot.
</callout>
<callout icon="🏅" color="gray_bg">
	**For FTC Competitors**
	Your team is already iterating between competitions. This project asks you to do the same work you would do anyway, but with discipline: change one thing, measure it, write down what happened. Coordinate testing time with your team, and avoid tying up the competition robot in the week before an event. Your engineering notebook entries from build sessions are the raw material for this project, so keep them current.
</callout>
<callout icon="🔬" color="gray_bg">
	**Project 4 vs. Project 6**
	In Project 4, you verify your subsystem against its requirements: does it pass or fail, and what will you change to make it pass? In Project 6, you investigate how a system behaves: how does one variable affect another? Project 4 is about closing the gap to a requirement. Project 6 is about understanding why the gap exists in the first place.
</callout>
### Part 1: Verification Test Plan
*Goal: Decide exactly how you will test your subsystem before you run a single trial.*
1. **Performance Requirements**<br>List the performance requirements and MOPs from your Requirements document. If your subsystem changed since Project 1, update the requirements now and note what changed and why. Choose at least two performance requirements to verify; one of them should be the requirement you think your subsystem is most likely to fail.
	<empty-block/>
2. **Test procedure**<br>For each requirement, write a procedure that a teammate could follow. Identify how many trials are requires, what is being measured and how, what is being held constant, and what counts as a valid trial.
	<empty-block/>
3. **Data table**<br>Build a data table for each test before you collect any data, with trial numbers, labeled columns, and units.
	<empty-block/>
4. **Potential Sources of error**<br>Predict what could make your measurements inaccurate or inconsistent, and how you will minimize each one.
<empty-block/>
<callout icon="2️⃣" color="gray_bg">
	**For ME2 (Advanced option for ME1)**
	1. **Formal test plan**<br>For each requirement, specify the MOP being tested, the test setup with diagrams, the acceptance threshold, and how results will be analyzed (which statistics you will calculate and how you will judge pass or fail).
	2. **Trial number rationale**<br>Justify the number of trials based on how much your subsystem varies and the consequence of a false pass. A requirement that is a KDR, or one where a false pass could cost a match, needs more trials than one that doesn't.
</callout>
<empty-block/>
### Part 2: Baseline Verification
*Goal: Find out, with evidence, where your subsystem stands right now.*
1. **Run the tests**<br>Run each test as written. If you have to change a procedure, record what changed and why in your engineering notebook.
	<empty-block/>
2. **Record every trial**<br>Record every trial, including the ones that look wrong. If you believe a trial was invalid (the robot was bumped, an artifact was damaged), keep it in the table and explain in a Notes column.
	<empty-block/>
3. **Verification checklist**<br>For each requirement, state whether your subsystem passes, fails, or is inconclusive relative to its threshold, with the evidence that supports your judgment. This is your **baseline**: the starting point every later iteration is compared to.
<empty-block/>
<callout icon="2️⃣" color="gray_bg">
	**For ME2 (Advanced option for ME1)**
	1. **Statistics**<br>Calculate the mean and standard deviation for each set of repeated trials. Judge not only whether the mean passes the threshold, but whether the spread of results passes.
	2. **Marginal results**<br>When a result is inconclusive or close to the threshold, identify whether the uncertainty comes from the subsystem's variability, from measurement error, or both.
	3. **Test method**<br>Evaluate whether your measurement method introduces systematic error, and propose a more accurate method if one exists.
</callout>
<empty-block/>
### Part 3: Iterate
*Goal: Move your subsystem's measured performance toward a requirement it doesn't yet meet, one documented change at a time.*
1. **Choose a target**<br>From your verification checklist, choose one requirement your subsystem fails, passes inconsistently, or passes with very little margin. State the requirement, its threshold, and your baseline measurement. Your target must have a number: "make the intake better" is not a target, "intake 3 artifacts in under 2.0 s in 9 of 10 trials" is.
	<empty-block/>
2. **Symptom and cause**<br>Describe what you observed (the symptom) separately from what you believe is causing it (the cause). 
	Example symptom: "The arm sags at full extension" is a symptom. 
	Example cause: "The 3D printed bracket flexes under the arm's weight"
	<empty-block/>
3. **Change one thing at a time**<br>Make one change, then re-run your test. For each iteration, record:
	- the observation or test result that motivated the change, and what you believe is causing it
	- what you changed
	- the measured result
	- whether the result was an improvement, no change, or a regression, and what you will try next because of it
	If you change more than one thing at once (this happens in a real build), say so: you will not be able to tell which change caused the result. Your iteration log should be built from your engineering notebook entries and your Onshape version history. Cite or link the specific entries and versions.
	<empty-block/>
4. **Progress graph**<br>Graph your target measurement after every iteration, with the requirement's threshold drawn as a horizontal line. Label your axes and units. This graph should make it obvious, at a glance, whether you are getting closer.
	<empty-block/>
<callout icon="🐞" color="gray_bg">
	**Repairing is not iterating**
	Replacing a broken part with an identical copy, tightening a loose set screw, or re-gluing a belt is repair. It counts as an iteration only when you change the design to move a measured value toward a requirement. If you discover a build deficiency, fix it, note it, and re-establish your baseline before you start iterating.
</callout>
<callout icon="2️⃣" color="gray_bg">
	**For ME2 (advanced option for ME1)**
	1. **Hypothesis and prediction**<br>Before each change, state what you believe is causing the gap, the evidence that would prove or disprove that belief, and the result you expect if you are right. After testing, compare the measured result to your prediction. A result that surprises you is evidence too, so record what it tells you.<br>Ex. "The intake stalls on the second artifact. Hypothesis: roller compression is too high, so the motor stalls when two artifacts are in contact. Prediction: reducing compression from 8 mm to 5 mm will eliminate the stall in 10 of 10 trials without dropping artifacts."
	2. **Progress tracking (TPM)**<br>If you set a TPM in Project 1, use it here: plot your predicted value alongside your measured value at every iteration. After each iteration, state whether the subsystem is closer to the threshold, further from it, or stuck.
	3. **Pivot or persist**<br>If your tracking shows you are stuck, or your evidence shows the current approach can't reach the threshold (the motor is already at stall, the material is already at its limit), make an explicit decision: pivot to a different approach, or continue with a written justification for why more iteration will close the gap. Either decision is acceptable. Drifting without deciding is not.
</callout>
### Part 4: Final Verification
*Goal: State where your subsystem ended up, compared to where it started.*
1. **Baseline vs. Final**<br>Compare your final result to your baseline. How much did your target measurement change, and which iteration(s) were responsible for most of the change?
<callout icon="✂️" color="gray_bg">
	**If your subsystem gets cut**
	Teams cut subsystems all the time, and often for good reasons. If your subsystem is removed from the robot during this project, you can still complete Project 4 with your iteration log up to that point. Replace Part 4 with a **close-out summary**: what you learned, what the limiting factor was, and what would need to be different for the approach to succeed. Frame the cut as a finding, not a failure. (Required for ME2 Advanced; strongly recommended for everyone.)
</callout>
### Reflection
Write a few paragraphs (roughly 300 to 500 words) in your reflection document reflecting on your Project 4 process. Cite specific engineering notebook entries as evidence for your claims; don't just summarize from memory.
At minimum, reflect on the following:
1. **The change that mattered most.** Which iteration had the biggest effect on your target measurement? What evidence led you to try it?
2. **A change that didn't work.** Describe an iteration that produced no change or a regression. What did it teach you about the cause of the problem?
3. **Symptom vs. cause.** Was there a point where you were treating a symptom instead of its cause? How did you figure that out?
<callout icon="2️⃣" color="gray_bg">
	**ME2 Students**
	**Your predictions.** How often did your measured results match your predictions? When they didn't, what did that tell you about your understanding of the subsystem?
</callout>

### Rubric
#### Iteration
<columns>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Proficient**
		- Identifies and documents the specific observation or test result that motivated design changes, distinguishing between what was observed (the symptom) and what caused it (the root cause).
		- Changes one thing at a time where possible, so the effect of each change can be attributed clearly; when multiple changes are made simultaneously, acknowledgment that the individual contributions cannot be separated.
		- Records the result of each change as an improvement, no change, or a regression, and uses this to decide what to change next.
	</column>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Advanced**
		In addition to Proficient…
		- Systematic failure diagnosis: before changing anything, a hypothesis is generated, with the evidence that would prove or disprove it.
		- Each iteration designed as a test of a specific hypothesis, with the result that would confirm or refute it documented in advance, and the outcome evaluated against that prediction.
		- Progress toward requirements tracked across iterations: after each build cycle, an explicit assessment of whether the subsystem is closer to, further from, or stuck relative to the relevant MOP or functional requirement.
		- Recognition when an approach is fundamentally limited (when continued iteration is unlikely to close the gap to requirements), with an explicit decision to either pivot to a different approach or formally justify continuing.
	</column>
</columns>
#### Testing
<columns>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Proficient**
		- A test procedure designed before conducting the test, identifying what is being measured, what is being held constant, and what constitutes a meaningful trial, recorded in enough detail that a teammate could replicate it.
		- Probable sources of error in the measurements identified.
		- Enough trials to generate a valid conclusion, with all trials recorded, not just successful ones.
		- Data collected and organized in a clearly structured table with labeled columns, units, and trial numbers.
		- A graph of results with correctly labeled axes and units, identifying the trend or relationship shown by the data.
		- Test results connected to the relevant requirement, with an explicit statement of whether the subsystem passed, failed, or produced inconclusive results relative to the specified threshold.
	</column>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Advanced**
		In addition to Proficient…
		- A formal test plan written before conducting any trials, specifying the MOP being tested, the test setup, the number of trials and justification for that number, the acceptance threshold, and how results will be analyzed.
		- Mean and standard deviation calculated for repeated trials, used to characterize the reliability of the result: not just whether the mean passes, but whether the distribution of results passes.
		- An evaluation of the accuracy of the test method itself, identifying whether the measurement approach introduces systematic error and proposing a more accurate method if one exists.
		- For subsystem testing specifically: sample size justified based on observed variability and the consequence of a false pass, connected directly to the MOP specification in the requirements. When results are inconclusive or marginal, explicit identification of whether the uncertainty is due to subsystem variability, measurement error, or both.
	</column>
</columns>
#### Technical Communication
<columns>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Proficient**
		- Written explanations that connect a design decision, test result, or prediction to the underlying engineering reasoning, requirement, or physics, using accurate technical vocabulary.
		- A clear, specific claim about whether a design meets a requirement, supported by evidence such as test data, CAD verification, or calculations.
		- Evidence that is relevant and sufficient to support the claim, including quantitative evidence (measurements, calculations, statistics) where available.
		- Because technical writing is a developing skill, proficient work successfully communicates the claim and evidence, but may contain minor errors in organization, clarity, or vocabulary use.
	</column>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Advanced**
		- Explanations and arguments that are logically coherent, using clear, quantitative reasoning to connect evidence to the claim rather than asserting a conclusion.
		- Quantitative reasoning used to reflect on the strength of the claim, including its limitations, sources of uncertainty, or where more evidence would be needed.
		- Writing that is concise and efficient, communicating complex engineering ideas at a level of depth and technicality appropriate to the intended audience (a teammate, a judge, a general reader).
		- Clear multi-paragraph structure that separates distinct ideas, design intent, evidence, and conclusion, rather than blending them together.
	</column>
</columns>
