Computer-aided Design or CAD is used across almost every field of engineering.  CAD is not just a drawing tool, it is how engineers think through a design before committing to material and machine time. A CAD model lets an engineer test whether parts fit, whether an assembly moves the way it should, and whether a design satisfies its requirements, all before anything gets cut, printed, or ordered. In industry, CAD models are often the basis of complex physics simulations that test the expected physical properties of the design such as its strength, flexibility, air resistance, thermal conductivity, etc.
Just as importantly, CAD is a communication tool. A well-built model and its drawings let an engineer hand off a design to a teammate, a manufacturer, or a reviewer who can evaluate it without needing the original designer in the room. The same is true for your FTC subsystem. As you work through these tutorials, you are building both skills at once: modeling a subsystem accurately enough to catch problems before fabrication, and documenting it clearly enough that your teammates and coaches can use it for feedback and fabrication.
In Avenues Robotics we use OnShape, a professional-level cloud-based CAD program that allows multiple teammates to access documents simultaneously and uses version control to track your design's revision history. These tutorials build proficiency in Onshape from the ground up: the first several teach part creation using progressively more advanced tools, the next few move into assembling multiple parts together, and the last two apply those skills directly to FTC hardware. If you already have Onshape experience, you can move quickly through the early part-modeling tutorials and focus your time on assemblies (T6 onward). If you're new to Onshape, work through the sequence in order.
