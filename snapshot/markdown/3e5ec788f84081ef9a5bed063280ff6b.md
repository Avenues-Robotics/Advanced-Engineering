Every FTC season starts the same way. The game is revealed, the team gets excited, and everyone has ideas. Two weeks later, rookies are building a drivetrain, CADers are changing the plan, programmers are waiting on hardware that doesn't exist yet, and no one is quite sure what got decided at the last meeting.
This isn't a skills problem. It's a coordination problem. **Project management** is how engineering teams solve it: deciding what to work on, in what order, who owns it, and how everyone knows where things stand.
This reading covers:
1. **Two approaches to project management**, and where each one comes from
2. **Why FTC teams need a hybrid** of the two approaches
3. **How our season is structured:** phases, gates, and sprints
4. **The tools we use every practice:** the backlog, the board, task cards, and stand-ups
---
## Two Approaches to Project Management
### <span underline="true">Phase-gated</span>: plan carefully, then build
For most of engineering history, big projects followed what's often called the **waterfall** model. You write the requirements, then do the design, then build, then test. Each phase flows into the next like water down a series of steps.
Modern versions add **gates** between phases: formal reviews where the team has to show that the work is ready before moving on. NASA's project life cycle works this way. A spacecraft design passes through a series of reviews before anyone builds flight hardware, because discovering a problem after launch is catastrophic.
Phase-gated work is at its best when **change is expensive**. In NASAs case, if a mistake costs months and millions of dollars to fix, it's worth spending time up front to get it right.  The FTC equivalent is if a change requires a month to overhaul the entire robot, order new parts and remanufacture expensive metal components, then its worth taking an extra week or two to get it right the first time.
### <span underline="true">Agile</span>: build in short cycles and adjust
In 2001, a group of software developers frustrated with waterfall wrote the [**Agile Manifesto**](https://agilemanifesto.org/). Their insight was simple: when you can't know everything up front, work in short cycles, get something working, learn from it, and adjust. Instead of one giant plan, you make a series of small plans, each informed by what you learned in the last one.
Agile is at its best when **change is cheap**. Rewriting a function in software can happen in between practice sessions.  Reprinting a claw device takes an hour. If trying something and learning from it costs less than planning it perfectly, you should try it.
<callout icon="💡" color="blue_bg">
	Neither approach is "better." Each fits a different kind of problem. Knowing which one fits the situation in front of you is the real engineering skill.
</callout>

## Why FTC Teams Need a Hybrid
An FTC robot sits in an awkward middle ground.
**Some of it is expensive to change.** Rebuilding a mechanism costs parts, machine time, and several practice sessions. For a team that meets a few hours a week, one bad rebuild can eat a month of the season. That argues for careful up-front design.
**Some of it is cheap to change, and much of it is uncertain.** Code can be rewritten quickly. Prototypes can be built from cardboard in an afternoon. And no matter how well you plan, you'll learn things during the season you couldn't have known in September: a mechanism that worked in CAD jams on the field, another team reveals a better strategy, your first competition shows you can't score under defense. That argues for short cycles and adjusting as you go.
So we use both:
- **Early in the season, we think carefully before we build.** The team moves through design phases and passes a quick review (a gate) before committing to expensive work.
- **Once the robot exists, we iterate in short sprints.** Test, learn, fix, repeat, using testing and competition results as hard data.
- **All season long, we borrow Agile's coordination tools:** a shared board, clear ownership of tasks, stand-ups, and retrospectives. These keep a large team with changing attendance working together.
If someone says our team "runs Agile," that's only half the story. We run a hybrid, on purpose.

### Iterate cheaply before you commit
Careful up-front design doesn't mean no experimenting. It means experimenting where it's cheap. In phases 1 and 2, fail fast in cardboard, wood, 3D prints, and quick test rigs. A prototype that teaches you your concept won't work has done its job. Save metal and CNC time for designs that have passed a gate.
### Gate reviews
A gate review is short, about 15 minutes. The subteam presents its work, and a mentor or experienced member asks hard questions:
- Does this design meet our requirements?
- Did we genuinely compare alternatives, or did we pick our favorite and justify it afterward?
- Is it ready to build? Is the CAD complete, are parts available, and does someone own it?
If the answer is no, the team fixes the gap and comes back. Fifteen minutes at a gate can save weeks of rebuilding.
### Software can move faster
Code is cheap to change, so programmers don't need to wait for the mechanical phases. They can work in simulation or on a test chassis while the mechanical team is still in design, and run on a more purely Agile sprint rhythm from the start.

## Sprints
All season long, work is organized in **sprints of 2 to 3 weeks**. Each subteam shares one big goal per sprint and works together to get it done. Everyone can see what's happening on the board, and everyone knows what they need to do next. No dead time.

**Where the sprint goal comes from** depends on the time of year:
- **Phases 1 to 3:** the current phase sets the goal. Example: "Three intake concepts prototyped in cardboard and compared."
- **After Qual 1:** competition results set the goal. Example: "Cut shooter cycle time so we can score under defense."
**How long is a sprint?** Aim for 2 to 3 weeks, and let the season set the boundaries. End a sprint at a gate review or a competition whenever you can, even if that makes it a few days shorter or longer. A sprint that ends at a competition gets the best review there is.
Why a team goal instead of individual goals? Most members come to two sessions a week, so any one person only gets a few hours of work done in a sprint. A shared goal lets everyone's pieces add up to visible progress.

## The Backlog
The **backlog** is the team's master to-do list. If it's work, it goes here: build tasks, code, tests, fixes, learning tasks, portfolio work.
The backlog is different from the sprint's **To Do** list:
- **Backlog:** everything the team *might* do, someday
- **To Do:** what the team committed to *this sprint*
**Anyone can add a card.** Noticed a loose bolt? Have an idea? Found a bug? Add it to the backlog so it doesn't get lost.
**Leads keep it in priority order.** Most important tasks go at the top of the backlog. "Everything is important" is not an order. Ordering the backlog forces the team to make real decisions about what matters.
**Cards can start rough.** A backlog card can be one line. Before it moves into a sprint, leads break it into session-sized tasks and add a definition of done.
**At the sprint turnover,** pull cards from the top of the backlog into the sprint and mark them To Do. Take less than you think you can finish. Teams almost always overestimate what they can do, and finishing early is a great problem to have.
**Clean it up each sprint.** Delete cards that no longer matter. A backlog of 200 old ideas is just noise.

## Good Task Cards
### The one session rule
Every task should be finishable in **one 90-minute practice**. If it's bigger, break it down. Session-sized tasks are a good check on whether your goal for the sprint is reasonable.  If you break “Build the intake” into 25 session-sized tasks you might realize that your timeline doesn’t work for your goals and make adjustments.  Session-sized tasks also keep progress moving, make problems visible sooner, and smooth out the bumps created when people don't come every session.

### What every card needs
- **One owner.** Other people can help, but one name is on the card. A task owned by "everyone" is owned by no one.
- **A definition of done.** How you'll know it's finished. Without it, tasks sit at 90% complete for weeks.
- **A subsystem label in the title:** intake, drivetrain, auto, portfolio, and so on.
A good definition of done often comes straight from your requirements. If the shooter shall complete a firing cycle in 2 seconds or less, "Done when: cycle time is 2.0 s or less across 10 timed trials" is a clear finish line. Requirements say *what* the robot must do. The board is how the team gets there, one piece at a time.
### Learning is real work
On a team with lots of newer members, building skill is a big share of what the team produces. Treat it that way:
- Learning tasks go on the board like any other task. Mark them as **Learning**.
- **Pair up:** an experienced member owns the card, and a newer member learns alongside.
- The experienced member's job is to teach, not just to finish faster.
- Next time a similar task comes up, **the learner becomes the owner.**
### Handoff notes
Teammates come on different days. If you stop before a task is done, leave a comment on the card:
- What's done
- What's next
- Where the parts, files, or code are
The card becomes the baton, and the next person can pick it up without hunting you down.

## Stand-ups
At the start of every practice, each subteam does a **stand-up**: a five minute check-in where each person answers three questions.
1. What did I finish since last time?
2. What am I working on today?
3. Am I blocked?
The third question matters most. A stand-up exists to surface problems early. If the programmer can't test autonomous because the drivetrain isn't wired, the team needs to know today, not next week.
**"I don't know how to do this" counts as blocked.** Say it. Then find someone to pair with, or pick a different task.
**Don't let the stand-up turn into a discussion**. If an issue needs more than a minute, the people involved talk it through right after.
---
## The Sprint Turnover
At the last practice of each sprint, the subteam holds one combined meeting, about 30 minutes, in three steps. Doing them together means what you learn in the review flows straight into the next plan.
### 1. Review: what did we get done?
Check the work against the sprint goal. Show it on the robot whenever possible: drive it, run the mechanism, run the auto. Working hardware and code are the real measure of progress, not how busy everyone felt.
### 2. Retrospective (Retro): how did we work?
Ask one question about *how* the team worked, not what it built:
**"What's one thing we'll do differently next sprint?"**
Pick one. Actually do it. A retro that produces ten ideas and no action is just complaining with extra steps.
### 3. Plan: what's next?
Set the next sprint's goal, based on the current phase and what the review revealed. Pull cards from the top of the backlog, assign owners, and mark them To Do. Take less than you think you can finish.

## Common Pitfalls
- **The board goes stale.** Nobody updates it, so nobody trusts it, so nobody uses it. Update it at stand-up.
- **Building before the gate.** Excitement pushes teams to cut metal on an unreviewed design. Prototype cheaply instead.
- **Everything is priority one.** Leads decide the order, after hearing input.
- **Tasks sit at 90% for weeks.** Break them smaller and write a definition of done.
- **Skipping the retro.** It feels like the least urgent meeting. It's the one that makes every future sprint better.
- **Planning replaces building.** This system is meant to be lightweight. If managing the board takes more time than building the robot, simplify.
