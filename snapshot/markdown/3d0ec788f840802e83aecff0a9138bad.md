### Requirements
Every engineering project begins with a shared understanding of what the system must do and how well it must do it. Professional engineers write requirements as specific, testable statements so that anyone on the team, or someone brought in later, can verify unambiguously whether the design meets them. Requirements discipline is what keeps a team from discovering, late in a project, that they built the wrong thing well.
Proficient Requirements include:
- ConOps covering the basic anticipated operating scenarios from which requirements can be fully derived.
- Functional requirements stated as "shall" statements describing what the system must do, with one function per statement. Ex. "The arm shall extend to reach the high goal."
- Separate performance requirements as "shall" statements with a quantitative value specifying how well each function must be performed. Ex. "The arm shall extend to a minimum height of 900 mm."
- Shall statements specifying what one subsystem of a design requires from, or provides to, another subsystem at each physical, electrical, or data boundary between them (e.g., mounting interface, power/signal connection, timing or sequencing dependency).
- "Should" statements that identify desirable but non-mandatory goals, distinct from "shall" requirements.
- Specific enough detail that a peer could determine, after testing, whether each one was met or not.
Advanced Requirements also include:
- ConOps covering most anticipated operating scenarios from which requirements can be fully derived.
- Rationale statements on each requirement, capturing why the requirement exists and what assumption or objective it traces to. Ex. "The arm shall extend to a minimum height of 900 mm. High goal scoring yields 3x the points of low goal; arm reach is a Key Driving Requirement."
- Measures of Performance (MOPs) derived from performance requirements, the quantitative metrics used to verify the design meets each "shall." Ex. "Measured extension height ≥ 900 mm in 5 of 5 trials."
- Technical Performance Measures (TPMs), with predicted vs. actual values tracked across build iterations to surface deficiencies before they become failures.
- A completeness check on the requirement set: every function identified in the ConOps has at least one corresponding "shall" statement.
- A review of requirements for redundancy, removing or consolidating statements that duplicate one another.

### Conceptual Design
Before committing resources to a single approach, engineers generate and compare multiple candidate solutions, because the first idea is rarely the best one and a team that never compares alternatives has no way of knowing whether it chose well. A structured comparison, weighing each concept against the requirements rather than personal preference, is what turns a design choice into a defensible engineering decision.
Proficient Conceptual Design includes:
- Structured research to inform concept generation, documenting what has been tried before (existing robots, relevant mechanisms) and identifying which features are worth borrowing, adapting, or avoiding.
- At least three meaningfully distinct design concepts (not variations on the same idea), each plausibly satisfying the system requirements.
- Design concepts documented with an annotated sketch or CAD mock-up identifying key features, showing what the concept looks like and how it works at a functional level.
- Anticipated benefits and obvious limitations recorded for each concept.
- All concepts compared against each other using a decision matrix.
- A preferred concept selected, with the reason for the selection stated.
Advanced Conceptual Design also include:
- Each evaluation criterion in the decision matrix traced back to a specific requirement or ConOps scenario, so the selection rationale is grounded in the requirements rather than preference.
- Design concepts distinguished by identified risks, including technical risks (can we build this?), performance risks (will it meet the MOP?), and schedule risks (can we build it in time?).
- Rationale for the baseline selection, showing it to be the most credible and well understood concept, not arbitrarily chosen.
- The final design decision documented with explicit reference to the trade study results, identified risks, and requirements.

### Detailed Design
A CAD model is more than a picture of a part, it is the artifact that communicates design intent to teammates, to a fabricator, and to the engineer's own future self. Professional engineers build models that are complete, organized, and verifiable against requirements before committing time and material to fabrication, because a mistake caught in CAD is far cheaper than one caught on the machine.
Proficient Detailed Design includes:
- A complete and well organized CAD model with all major components modeled or represented, assemblies mated correctly, and the design visually inspectable from any angle.
- The CAD document organized with sketches fully dimensioned, each part modeled and kept separate from other parts, and assemblies built distinctly from the individual part files.
- The CAD model checked against each functional requirement using a structured checklist: for each requirement, whether the design as modeled satisfies it, does not satisfy it, or cannot yet be determined.
Advanced Detailed Design also include:
- A complete bill of materials (BOM) in the CAD model with part names, quantities, and materials; key structural members and purchased components correctly specified.
- A pre-fabrication verification table, listing each "shall" requirement with the verification method (analysis, inspection of CAD, or similarity to tested design), the evidence from the CAD or calculation, and a pass/fail/TBD judgment.
- Requirements marked TBD identified, with documentation of what physical test or build artifact will be needed to close them.

### Fabrication
Translating a design from a model into a physical part is where tolerances, material behavior, and shop realities meet the engineer's intentions, and closing that gap safely and accurately is its own skill. Professional engineers select the right tool and process for the job, verify that what they built matches what they designed, and treat shop safety as a non-negotiable prerequisite to doing the work at all.
Safety prerequisite (pass/fail), required before using any power tool or fabrication equipment. The student must demonstrate:
- Correct PPE selection and use for each tool (safety glasses, hearing protection, etc.)
- Knowledge of the specific hazards associated with each tool they will use
- Proper body positioning, workholding, and feed direction
- Emergency stop procedures for each machine
A student who cannot demonstrate safe tool use does not proceed to fabrication. This is re-evaluated for each new tool category.
Proficient Fabrication includes:
- The appropriate tool selected for each fabrication task, with an explanation of why that tool is the right choice over alternatives available in the shop.
- Accurate use of all five core fabrication tools demonstrated:
	- Band saw: cuts to a line with visible kerf on the waste side; straight cuts within ±1mm of the intended dimension.
	- Drill press: correct bit diameter selected; perpendicular holes drilled at marked locations within ±1mm of position.
	- Dremel cutting disc: controlled cuts in confined geometry or on assembled components where the band saw cannot reach.
	- Laser cutter: drawing set up in the laser cutter software; correct power and speed selected for material.
	- 3D printer: model set up in slicer; parts oriented correctly for strength and minimal support; filament material, wall thickness, infill density, and infill pattern selected based on desired strength.
- Hand tools used correctly for assembly: correct fastener type and size selected, screws driven without stripping, and appropriate torque applied.
- Assembled components that fit together as designed; mating parts align correctly and move or hold as intended.
Advanced Fabrication also include:
- Fabricated parts measured against CAD dimensions using calipers, with documentation of whether each critical dimension is within tolerance.
- Fastener type and size selected based on the load and joint requirements, not just what is available, with the choice justified (e.g. thread-forming vs. machine screw, bolt length relative to material thickness).
- At least one part produced using CNC fabrication with teacher feedback: work coordinates set up, feeds and speeds selected for the material, and guidance incorporated on workholding, tabs, and toolpath settings before running the job; the finished part evaluated against the CAD model.

### Iteration
Engineering designs rarely work as intended on the first attempt, and the difference between productive iteration and wasted effort is discipline: changing one thing at a time, tracking what each change actually did, and using that evidence to decide what to try next. Professional engineers treat each build cycle as a test of a specific idea, not a random adjustment, so that progress toward the requirement is real and traceable.
Proficient Iteration:
- Identifies and documents the specific observation or test result that motivated design changes, distinguishing between what was observed (the symptom) and what caused it (the root cause).
- Changes one thing at a time where possible, so the effect of each change can be attributed clearly; when multiple changes are made simultaneously, acknowledgment that the individual contributions cannot be separated.
- Records the result of each change as an improvement, no change, or a regression, and uses this to decide what to change next.
Advanced Iteration also includes:
- Systematic failure diagnosis: before changing anything, at least two plausible hypotheses for the root cause generated, with the evidence that would distinguish between them identified.
- Each iteration designed as a test of a specific hypothesis, with the result that would confirm or refute it documented in advance, and the outcome evaluated against that prediction.
- Progress toward requirements tracked across iterations: after each build cycle, an explicit assessment of whether the subsystem is closer to, further from, or stuck relative to the relevant MOP or functional requirement.
- Recognition when an approach is fundamentally limited (when continued iteration is unlikely to close the gap to requirements), with an explicit decision to either pivot to a different approach or formally justify continuing.

### Testing
A requirement is only a claim until it has been verified against evidence, and verifying it well requires more than running the system once and eyeballing the result. Professional engineers design tests that isolate what they are measuring, collect enough data to trust the conclusion, and make an explicit, evidence based judgment about whether the design passed, failed, or needs more information.
Proficient Testing includes:
- A test procedure designed before conducting the test, identifying what is being measured, what is being held constant, and what constitutes a meaningful trial, recorded in enough detail that a teammate could replicate it.
- Probable sources of error in the measurements identified.
- Enough trials to generate a valid conclusion, with all trials recorded, not just successful ones.
- Data collected and organized in a clearly structured table with labeled columns, units, and trial numbers.
- A graph of results with correctly labeled axes and units, identifying the trend or relationship shown by the data.
- Test results connected to the relevant requirement, with an explicit statement of whether the subsystem passed, failed, or produced inconclusive results relative to the specified threshold.
Advanced Testing also include:
- A formal test plan written before conducting any trials, specifying the MOP being tested, the test setup, the number of trials and justification for that number, the acceptance threshold, and how results will be analyzed.
- Mean and standard deviation calculated for repeated trials, used to characterize the reliability of the result: not just whether the mean passes, but whether the distribution of results passes.
- An evaluation of the accuracy of the test method itself, identifying whether the measurement approach introduces systematic error and proposing a more accurate method if one exists.
- For subsystem testing specifically: sample size justified based on observed variability and the consequence of a false pass, connected directly to the MOP specification in the requirements. When results are inconclusive or marginal, explicit identification of whether the uncertainty is due to subsystem variability, measurement error, or both.
- For a controlled experiment specifically: a function fit to the data and the correlation coefficient calculated, used to characterize the strength of the relationship between variables; uncertainty in individual measurements quantified and propagated to summary statistics.

### Mechanics
A design decision backed only by intuition is a guess, while one backed by a physics-based calculation is an engineering judgment that can be checked, defended, and improved. Professional engineers connect the physical principles of force, motion, and energy directly to their design choices, quantifying margins so that risk is understood rather than assumed.
Proficient Mechanics includes:
- At least one physics analysis directly tied to a design decision with work shown in units and assumptions stated explicitly.
	- Examples: required motor torque to lift or move a known load, gear ratio selection, structural member sizing; joint reach to satisfy a height or extension requirement, range of motion analysis.
- Concepts at this level: force (N), torque (N·m), mass (kg), weight (N), distance / displacement (m), angle (degrees or radians), gear ratio (unitless), mechanical advantage (unitless), velocity (m/s), and rotational speed (RPM or rad/s).
Advanced Mechanics also include:
- A connected chain of physics analysis covering the primary load path or control loop for a subsystem, not just one calculation, but a sequence where the output of one step is the input to the next.
	- Examples: motor torque to gear reduction to output force to factor of safety on structure; input motion to linkage geometry to end effector position and velocity.
- A factor of safety calculated for at least one critical element: the worst-case load identified, the rated capacity of the material or component determined, the ratio computed, and an explicit judgment of whether the margin is acceptable given the consequence of failure.
- Concepts at this level (builds on Level 1): acceleration (m/s²), angular acceleration (rad/s²), moment of inertia (kg·m²), normal force (N), friction force (N), coefficient of friction (unitless), tension (N), compression (N), shear force (N), bending moment (N·m), stress (Pa), factor of safety (unitless), rated load / capacity (N or N·m), work (J), and power (W).

### Documentation
An engineering decision that exists only in someone's memory is a liability to the team and a missed opportunity to learn from it later. Professional engineers keep a running record of what changed, why it changed, and what evidence drove the change, so that teammates can pick up where they left off and the reasoning behind a design can be reconstructed months or years later.
Proficient Documentation includes:
- A running engineering log with an entry for each meaningful development of a design; each entry records the date, what was changed, and what prompted the change (a design decision, a test result, an observation, a measurement, or a peer suggestion).
- Entries detailed enough that a teammate who missed the build session could understand what happened and why, with individual contributions attributed so it is clear who did what.
- Reflection on the design process of each major phase (requirements, conceptual design, detailed design, fabrication, testing, competition): what went well, what didn't, and what they would do differently.
- Regular personal learning reflection: what was learned, what was hard, what was surprising, and what can be improved.
Advanced Documentation also include:
- Log entries that include not just what changed and why, but which alternative fixes were considered and why the chosen approach was selected over others, connected to a specific requirement, MOP, or ConOps scenario where applicable.
- When a design is modified in response to a test finding, an explicit diagnosis of whether the finding indicated a design problem, a build quality problem, or a requirements problem, recorded before the fix is described.
- Log and all documentation maintained in a shared digital project management tool (e.g. GitHub Projects) so all team members have visibility into the current state and history of each subsystem.
- Phase reflections that go beyond process, including an honest assessment of the quality of the engineering decisions, not just what happened, but whether they were good choices and why or why not.
- Personal learning reflections that identify specific skills or concepts you are actively working to improve, with progress on those goals tracked across entries as a narrative of growth rather than isolated observations.

### Technical Communication
An engineering result that cannot be explained clearly to someone else has limited value, whether the audience is a teammate, a judge, a client, or a future version of the engineer who has forgotten the details. Professional engineers write and speak about their work in a way that lays out the claim, the evidence behind it, and an honest account of what did and did not work.
Proficient Technical Communication includes:
- Written explanations that connect a design decision, test result, or prediction to the underlying engineering reasoning, requirement, or physics, using accurate technical vocabulary.
- A clear, specific claim about whether a design meets a requirement, supported by evidence such as test data, CAD verification, or calculations.
- Evidence that is relevant and sufficient to support the claim, including quantitative evidence (measurements, calculations, statistics) where available.
- Because technical writing is a developing skill, proficient work successfully communicates the claim and evidence, but may contain minor errors in organization, clarity, or vocabulary use.
Advanced Technical Communication also includes:
- Explanations and arguments that are logically coherent, using clear, quantitative reasoning to connect evidence to the claim rather than asserting a conclusion.
- Quantitative reasoning used to reflect on the strength of the claim, including its limitations, sources of uncertainty, or where more evidence would be needed.
- Writing that is concise and efficient, communicating complex engineering ideas at a level of depth and technicality appropriate to the intended audience (a teammate, a judge, a general reader).
- Clear multi-paragraph structure that separates distinct ideas, design intent, evidence, and conclusion, rather than blending them together.
