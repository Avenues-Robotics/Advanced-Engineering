## Overview
Every decision in robot code rests on assumptions about how the code and the hardware will perform. A controller you write yourself will beat the one built into the hardware. Reading a sensor every loop doesn’t affect performance. The distance sensor reports the true distance. Each of these sounds reasonable, and most never get measured. When one is wrong, the result is code that is more complicated than it needs to be, or a robot that performs worse than it could, and nobody can explain why.
<callout icon="💡" color="gray_bg">
	**Example: Built-in servo control vs. custom PID**
	A team wants a more precise arm. They switch the arm servo to continuous rotation mode, add position feedback, and write a PID controller in their OpMode, assuming their own controller will outperform the one inside the servo. It might. But the servo's built-in controller reads its own position sensor directly and was tuned by the people who designed it, while the team's controller can only react as fast as the OpMode loop runs and is only as good as the team's tuning. Until the team measures settling time and position error for both approaches, they cannot know whether they made the arm better, made it worse, or added complexity for no gain.
</callout>
In this project, you turn one of those assumptions into a measured fact. You will design and run a controlled experiment, analyze the results, and write them up as a technical report in the format engineers and scientists use to publish their work.
You have practiced the scientific method in science class. This project applies the same method to your robot and adds the question that makes the result useful to an engineer: what does this mean for the code?
<callout icon="🔬" color="gray_bg">
	**Project 4 vs. Project 6**
	In Project 4, you verified your subsystem against its requirements: does it pass or fail? In Project 6, you investigate how a the system behaves: how does one variable affect another? The answer often explains why a subsystem passes in the lab and fails on the field, or tells you what a requirement should have said in the first place.
</callout>
<callout icon="🏅" color="gray_bg">
	**For FTC Competitors**
	Choose a question your team actually needs answered. The best experiments come from problems you have already run into: an autonomous routine that drifts, a sensor reading you don't trust, a shooter that misses when the battery runs low. Coordinate robot time with your team, and avoid tying up the competition robot in the week before an event.
</callout>
### Part 1: Research Question and Variables
Goal: define exactly what you are investigating, and what you will change, measure, and hold constant.
1. **Research question**<br>Write a question in the form "How does \[independent variable\] affect \[dependent variable\]?" Choose a question you can answer with the robot, tools, and time you have. Use one of the example questions below or propose your own.
2. **Background**<br>Explain why the question matters for your robot. Identify the code, subsystem, or requirement that depends on the answer, and connect it to your Project 1 requirements or Project 4 code where you can.
3. **Variables**<br>Identify each of the following:
	- the **independent variable (IV)**: what you deliberately change, and the values (levels) you will test
	- the **dependent variable (DV)**: what you measure, its units, and how you will measure it
	- the **controlled variables (CVs)**: everything else that could affect the DV, and how you will hold each one constant
4. **Hypothesis**<br>Predict the result and explain the reasoning behind your prediction. A hypothesis is a claim that could turn out to be wrong. "Error will increase with distance because the reflected signal gets weaker as distance increases" is a hypothesis. "Distance will affect error" is not.

	- **IV:** actual distance from the sensor face to the target, measured with a tape measure: 10, 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 cm
	- **DV:** measurement error, calculated as the sensor reading minus the actual distance (cm)

### Part 2: Test Plan
Goal: write a plan detailed enough that a teammate could run your experiment without asking you anything.
The <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/401ec788f84081e38054deaae6f6d54c"/> reading treats every gain change as a controlled experiment. It is a useful model for the plan you write here, even if your question has nothing to do with PID.
1. **Setup**<br>Describe the hardware, the OpMode you will run, and any fixtures or measuring tools. Include a photo or labeled sketch.
2. **Procedure**<br>Write numbered steps for a single trial, from setup to recording the result. Define what counts as a valid trial, and what you will do if something goes wrong partway through one.
3. **Plan parts**<br>Each part of your plan changes one IV and measures its effect on the DV. If your investigation has more than one IV, or more than one pairing of IV and DV, write a separate part for each. In every part, state the fixed value of each IV you are not testing.
4. **Trials**<br>State how many levels of the IV you will test and how many trials you will run at each level. To see a relationship, test at least five levels. To see how much the result varies on its own, run at least three trials at each level.
5. **Data table**<br>Build your data table before you collect any data, with trial numbers, labeled columns, and units.
6. **Sources of error**<br>Predict what could make your measurements inaccurate or inconsistent, and how you will minimize each one.

	**For SCS2 (advanced option for SCS1)**
	1. **Formal test plan**<br>State the acceptance threshold your results will be judged against (from a requirement or MOP where one exists), the number of trials with a justification for that number, and your analysis plan: which statistics you will calculate and what kind of function you expect to fit.
	2. **Log file design**<br>Plan for your OpMode to write data to a log file rather than recording values by hand. Specify the columns (always include a timestamp), how often a row is written, and a file naming scheme that traces every file to a part, an IV level, and a trial.
	3. **Measurement accuracy**<br>Identify how your measurement method could introduce systematic error (a reading that is always too high or too low by about the same amount), and how you will check for it.
</callout>
### Part 3: Plan Review
Goal: catch problems in your plan before they cost you a day of data.
1. **Get feedback**<br>Bring your Part 1 and Part 2 work to your one-on-one meeting or submit it for written feedback. Do not begin collecting data until your plan has been reviewed.
2. **Revise**<br>Update your plan in response to the feedback. Keep a short change note: what feedback you received, what you changed, and anything you chose not to change and why.
3. **Pilot run**<br>Run two or three trials to confirm that the procedure works: the robot does what you expect, your measurement method is practical, and your data table has every column you need. Label these as pilot trials and leave them out of your analysis. If the pilot reveals a problem, fix the plan and add the change to your change note.
### Part 4: Collect Data
Goal: produce a complete and honest record of every trial.
1. **Follow the plan**<br>Run each part of your plan as written. If you have to change the procedure, record what changed and why in your engineering notebook.
2. **Record every trial**<br>Record every trial, including the ones that look wrong. If you believe a trial was invalid (the robot was bumped, the target moved), keep it in the table and explain in the Notes column. An unexpected result is information; deleting it hides that information.
3. **Record conditions**<br>Record anything that could explain variation between trials: battery voltage at the start of each set, date and time, and the robot's configuration.
<callout icon="2️⃣" color="gray_bg">
	**For SCS2 (advanced option for SCS1)**
	1. **Write log files**<br>Your OpMode writes each trial's data to a .csv file on the Robot Controller as the trial runs. Transfer the files to your laptop, keep the raw files unedited, and build your summary table from them. A log file records every value your code sees, many times per second, with no copying errors. A single trial can produce hundreds of readings, which lets you measure how much a value varies within a trial, not only between trials.
	2. **Archive**<br>Store all log files in one folder (or in your team's GitHub repository) and link it in your report's appendix.
</callout>
### Part 5: Analyze
Goal: turn your data into evidence that answers your research question.
1. **Summary table**<br>For each level of the IV, calculate the mean of your trials.
2. **Graph**<br>Graph the DV against the IV for each part of your plan, with a title, labeled axes, and units. Use a scatter plot for a numerical IV and a bar graph for a categorical IV.
3. **Describe the trend**<br>In a sentence or two, describe the relationship your graph shows. Does the DV increase, decrease, or stay about the same? Is the change steady, or does it change at some point?
4. **Compare**<br>Compare your results to your hypothesis. If your question connects to a requirement, state whether the results pass, fail, or are inconclusive relative to its threshold.
<callout icon="2️⃣" color="gray_bg">
	**For SCS2 (advanced option for SCS1)**
	1. **Statistics**<br>Calculate the mean and standard deviation at each IV level, and show the standard deviation as error bars on your graph. Judge not only whether the mean passes the threshold, but whether the spread of results passes.
	2. **Curve fit**<br>Fit a function to your data (linear, or another form your reasoning supports). Report its equation and the correlation coefficient (r), and explain what the fit means for your code. Ex. A linear fit of sensor error vs. distance gives you a correction your code can apply to every reading.
	3. **Uncertainty**<br>Estimate the uncertainty in your individual measurements (tape measure resolution, sensor resolution, timing) and carry it through to your summary statistics.
	4. **Test method**<br>Evaluate whether your measurement method introduced systematic error, and propose a more accurate method if one exists.
</callout>
### Part 6: Technical Report
Goal: communicate your experiment so that a reader can understand it, trust it, and repeat it.
Write your report in the format used by professional engineering and science journals. Each section has one job. Keeping each kind of content in its own section is what makes a report easy to read and easy to check.

### Reflection

At minimum, reflect on the following:

2. **A surprise.** What result surprised you, and what did it teach you about your robot?
3. **Your method.** If you ran this experiment again, what would you change about your method, and why?

### Rubric
#### Testing
<columns>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Proficient**
		- A test procedure designed before conducting the test, identifying what is being measured, what is being held constant, and what constitutes a meaningful trial, recorded in enough detail that a teammate could replicate it.
		- Probable sources of error in the measurements identified.
		- Enough trials to generate a valid conclusion, with all trials recorded, not just successful ones.
		- Data collected and organized in a clearly structured table with labeled columns, units, and trial numbers.
		- A graph of results with correctly labeled axes and units, identifying the trend or relationship shown by the data.
		- Test results connected to the relevant requirement, with an explicit statement of whether the subsystem passed, failed, or produced inconclusive results relative to the specified threshold.
	</column>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Advanced**
		In addition to Proficient…
		- A formal test plan written before conducting any trials, specifying the MOP being tested, the test setup, the number of trials and justification for that number, the acceptance threshold, and how results will be analyzed.
		- Mean and standard deviation calculated for repeated trials, used to characterize the reliability of the result: not just whether the mean passes, but whether the distribution of results passes.
		- An evaluation of the accuracy of the test method itself, identifying whether the measurement approach introduces systematic error and proposing a more accurate method if one exists.
		- For subsystem testing specifically: sample size justified based on observed variability and the consequence of a false pass, connected directly to the MOP specification in the requirements. When results are inconclusive or marginal, explicit identification of whether the uncertainty is due to subsystem variability, measurement error, or both.
		- For a controlled experiment specifically: a function fit to the data and the correlation coefficient calculated, used to characterize the strength of the relationship between variables; uncertainty in individual measurements quantified and propagated to summary statistics.
	</column>
</columns>
#### Technical Communication
<columns>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Proficient**
		- Written explanations that connect a design decision, test result, or prediction to the underlying engineering reasoning, requirement, or physics, using accurate technical vocabulary.
		- A clear, specific claim about whether a design meets a requirement, supported by evidence such as test data, CAD verification, or calculations.
		- Evidence that is relevant and sufficient to support the claim, including quantitative evidence (measurements, calculations, statistics) where available.
		- Because technical writing is a developing skill, proficient work successfully communicates the claim and evidence, but may contain minor errors in organization, clarity, or vocabulary use.
	</column>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Advanced**
		In addition to Proficient…
		- Explanations and arguments that are logically coherent, using clear, quantitative reasoning to connect evidence to the claim rather than asserting a conclusion.
		- Quantitative reasoning used to reflect on the strength of the claim, including its limitations, sources of uncertainty, or where more evidence would be needed.
		- Writing that is concise and efficient, communicating complex engineering ideas at a level of depth and technicality appropriate to the intended audience (a teammate, a judge, a general reader).
		- Clear multi-paragraph structure that separates distinct ideas, design intent, evidence, and conclusion, rather than blending them together.
	</column>
</columns>
