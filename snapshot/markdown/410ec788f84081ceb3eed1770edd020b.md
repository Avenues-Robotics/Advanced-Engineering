### Overview
In this project you will choose one physics question about your robot, answer it with a physics model, and document your work clearly. Every student's question will be different, so you are assessed on the quality of your reasoning and documentation, not just your solution.
The best questions come from a real decision. Is this motor strong enough? How fast should this wheel turn? What is the torque required to lift the arm? If the answer would change something your team builds, tunes, or codes, it is a good question.
<callout icon="🏅" color="gray_bg">
	**For FTC Competitors**
	Use your own robot's numbers wherever you can: measured masses and dimensions, the motors and gearboxes you actually run, and manufacturer datasheets. An analysis built on real numbers is one your team can act on. An analysis built on invented numbers is a homework problem.
</callout>

### Part 2: Document Your Analysis
*Goal: make your analysis easy for someone else to follow, check, and use.*
Your physics report can be a typed document, handwritten on paper, a spreadsheet, and/or a Desmos/Jupyter notebook. Every report includes these sections:
1. **The question and why it matters**<br>What design decision does the answer inform? Which requirement or design choice does it trace to?
2. **Diagrams**<br>A labeled sketch of the system and a free-body diagram for any force or torque problem.
3. **Known values**<br>A table with each quantity, its value and units, and its source: measured, datasheet, or estimated.
4. **Assumptions**<br>List what you are ignoring or simplifying, with one sentence on why each is reasonable.
5. **Model and calculations**<br>Write equations in symbols first, then substitute numbers. Carry units through every step.
6. **Result and sanity check**<br>State the answer. Then check it: are the units right, is the size reasonable, does it behave sensibly at extreme values?
7. **Design implication**<br>What does the answer tell your team to do?
<callout icon="2️⃣" color="gray_bg">
	For ME2 (advanced option for ME1)
	1. **Uncertainty**<br>For your most important inputs, state how uncertain they are and how much that uncertainty changes your answer.
	2. **Check against reality**<br>Compare your prediction to a measurement on the robot or a testing rig.
	3. **Margin**<br>Calculate the factor of safety or design margin and judge whether it is enough, given what happens if it fails.
	4. **Limitations**<br>Name what your model leaves out and predict whether each would make the real answer higher or lower.
	5. **Typed equations**<br>Present equations as typed math (MathType, or LaTeX with Auto-LaTeX Equations in Docs).
</callout>
### Reflection
Write a few paragraphs (roughly 300 to 500 words) in your reflection document. Cite specific entries from your engineering notebook as evidence rather than summarizing from memory.
1. **What the physics told you.** Did your analysis confirm what your team already believed, or change it? What would have happened if you had gone with intuition alone?
2. **Where the model and reality disagreed.** What did your model get wrong or leave out, and how do you know?
3. **Next steps.** If you kept working on this question, what would you measure or model next, and why?

### Rubric
#### Mechanics
<columns>
	<column ratio="49.25">
		**Proficient**
		- At least one physics analysis directly tied to a design decision, with work shown in units and assumptions stated explicitly.
		- Concepts at this level: force, torque, mass, weight, displacement, angle, gear ratio, mechanical advantage, velocity, rotational speed, energy.
	</column>
	<column ratio="49.25">
		**Level 1 Advanced / Level 2 Proficient**
		In addition to Level 1 Proficient…
		- A connected chain of analysis where the output of one step is the input to the next, covering the primary load path or control loop.
		- A factor of safety for at least one critical element: worst-case load, rated capacity, the ratio, and a judgment of whether the margin is acceptable.
		- Adds concepts such as acceleration, moment of inertia, friction, stress, factor of safety, work, and power.
	</column>
</columns>
#### Technical Communication
<columns>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Proficient**
		- Written explanations that connect a design decision, test result, or prediction to the underlying engineering reasoning, requirement, or physics, using accurate technical vocabulary.
		- A clear, specific claim about whether a design meets a requirement, supported by evidence such as test data, CAD verification, or calculations.
		- Evidence that is relevant and sufficient to support the claim, including quantitative evidence (measurements, calculations, statistics) where available.
		- Because technical writing is a developing skill, proficient work successfully communicates the claim and evidence, but may contain minor errors in organization, clarity, or vocabulary use.
	</column>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Advanced**
		- Explanations and arguments that are logically coherent, using clear, quantitative reasoning to connect evidence to the claim rather than asserting a conclusion.
		- Quantitative reasoning used to reflect on the strength of the claim, including its limitations, sources of uncertainty, or where more evidence would be needed.
		- Writing that is concise and efficient, communicating complex engineering ideas at a level of depth and technicality appropriate to the intended audience (a teammate, a judge, a general reader).
		- Clear multi-paragraph structure that separates distinct ideas, design intent, evidence, and conclusion, rather than blending them together.
	</column>
</columns>
