A Request for Proposal (RFP) is a document that describes a problem and invites engineering teams to propose a solution. The person or organization who writes it is the <span color="red">**`customer`**</span>. The teams who respond are <span color="red">**`proposers`**</span>.
At its core, an RFP is a requirements document: a formal answer to two questions, *What does this system have to do?* and *How will we know if it worked?* What makes it unusual is who reads it. When you write requirements for a system you will design yourself, you can fill gaps from your own head. A proposer cannot. They only know what you write down, so you must describe the need clearly enough that a team you have never met could build something you would accept, without you telling them how to build it.
Real engineering organizations use RFPs constantly. NASA invites mission proposals through documents called Announcements of Opportunity. Cities issue RFPs for bridges and transit systems. Companies issue them when they need a part, a test fixture, or a piece of software built by someone else. In every case, the RFP is the contract between "what we need" and "what you will deliver."
A strong RFP answers four questions for the proposer:
1. **Why** does this project exist? (Mission need)
2. **What** must the system do, and how well? (Concept of Operations and requirements)
3. **What limits** must the solution live within? (Constraints, schedule, deliverables)
4. **How** will proposals be judged? (Evaluation criteria)
This reading uses a running example: a home-goods company writing an RFP for an **Automatic Pet Feeder**, a countertop product that dispenses measured portions of dry pet food on a schedule. The company is fictional. The engineering is real.

## Anatomy of an RFP
Most RFPs follow the same order, because each section depends on the one before it. A proposer reads top to bottom and should never meet a requirement whose purpose was not already explained above it.
1. **Background and Mission Need**: the problem, who has it, and why it matters now.
2. **Scope**: what is inside the project and what is explicitly outside it.
3. **Concept of Operations (ConOps)**: the scenarios the system must handle.
4. **Requirements**: shall and should statements, functional and performance.
5. **Constraints**: budget, materials, safety, rules, and anything else the solution must live within.
6. **Deliverables**: what the proposer must hand over, and in what form.
7. **Schedule and Milestones**: key dates and review gates.
8. **Evaluation Criteria**: how proposals will be compared and selected.
9. **Submission Instructions**: format, length, due date, and who to ask questions.
Sections 3 and 4 are the core of any requirements document, whether or not it is an RFP. The other sections are what turn a requirements document into a request that outside teams can answer and be judged on.

## Background, Mission Need, and Scope
The opening of an RFP answers one question: why should anyone build this? Proposers who understand the *why* make better design decisions on every detail you forgot to specify.
A <span color="red">**`mission need`**</span> statement describes the problem in terms of what someone cannot currently do. It does not describe a robot, a mechanism, or a sensor. If the need statement already names the solution, you have skipped the part of engineering where alternatives get considered.
<callout color="gray_bg">
	**Weak:** We need a feeder with a rotating auger and a phone app.
	**Strong:** Our current gravity feeder lets pets eat as much as they want. Owners of pets on a vet-recommended diet cannot use it, owners who travel cannot control when their pet eats, and determined pets knock it over to get days of food at once. Pet owners need a way to give measured portions on a schedule while away, without the pet getting extra food.
</callout>
Notice that the strong version gives the proposer real information: portions must be controlled, the owner may be away, and pets will actively try to defeat the product. Every one of those facts will shape a design.
<span color="red">**`Scope`**</span> draws the boundary of the project. It lists what the proposer is responsible for and, just as importantly, what they are not. Out-of-scope items prevent proposers from spending effort on things the customer will handle some other way.
<callout color="gray_bg">
	**In scope:** the feeder unit, its dispensing mechanism, electronics and firmware, a working prototype, and a quick-start guide.
	**Out of scope:** wet food, a smartphone app, packaging and branding, and safety certification testing.
</callout>

## Concept of Operations
Before writing a single requirement, you need to understand how the system will actually be used. That means writing down the scenarios the design has to handle: not just the easy ones, but the realistic range of situations it will encounter. This collection of scenarios is called a <span color="red">**`Concept of Operations`**</span>, or <span color="red">**`ConOps`**</span>. It describes what the system needs to do and under what conditions, before anyone commits to a design. Think of it as the answer to: *What is this system's job?*
Operating scenarios come from several sources:
- **The operating environment:** the physical conditions, infrastructure, and external constraints the system will face. A bridge must handle traffic loads, wind, thermal expansion, and earthquakes. A satellite must survive launch vibration, vacuum, and radiation that no lab fully replicates.
- **Stakeholders:** the people who will use, operate, or be affected by the system. A medical device engineer interviews surgeons and patients; an automotive engineer talks to drivers, mechanics, and safety regulators. Their expectations and habits define what the system must handle.
- **Known failure modes and edge cases:** past experience with similar systems and reasoning about what can go wrong. Aircraft designers write scenarios for engine failure not because they expect it, but because a system that only works when everything goes right is not a safe system.
In an RFP, the ConOps carries extra weight because the proposer was not in the room when you imagined these scenarios. Here is part of the ConOps for the pet feeder:
<callout color="gray_bg">
	**Setup:** The owner unboxes the feeder, fills it, sets a portion size and schedule, and walks away. No tools, account, or internet connection are needed.
	**Weekend away:** The owner leaves for 3 days. The feeder runs the schedule without anyone refilling or adjusting it.
	**Power outage (edge case):** Household power goes out overnight. The feeder keeps its schedule and settings.
	**Jam (edge case):** An oddly shaped piece of kibble blocks the dispenser. The feeder detects that food was not delivered and alerts the owner.
	**Tampering (edge case):** A large dog paws at the feeder and tries to knock it over to get more food.
</callout>
Every scenario here will generate at least one requirement. Notice that three of the five are edge cases. A feeder that works perfectly on a normal day but fails during a power outage on the owner's vacation fails the mission at exactly the moment it was needed.
Once the scenarios are written, the requirements should follow directly from them. If you find yourself writing a requirement that does not trace back to any scenario, ask whether it is really necessary. If your requirements do not cover every scenario, you have a gap.

## Requirements
Requirements come in a few types, and each is written in a specific way. The wording is precise on purpose: proposers will read every word literally.
### Functional requirements
A <span color="red">**`functional requirement`**</span> answers the question: *What must this system do?* It describes a function, an action the system must be capable of performing.
Functional requirements are written as <span color="red">**`shall`**</span> statements. The word *shall* is not a stylistic choice. It signals a mandatory requirement: if the system does not do this, it fails.
<callout color="gray_bg">
	1. The feeder **shall** store dry pet food.
	2. The feeder **shall** dispense a set portion of food at each scheduled time.
	3. The feeder **shall** detect when a scheduled portion was not dispensed.
	4. The feeder **shall** keep its schedule and settings when household power is lost.
	5. The feeder **shall** prevent the pet from reaching stored food.
</callout>
Each statement describes one function. **One function per statement** is the rule, not because it is a formatting preference, but because it makes requirements testable. If a requirement describes two functions, you cannot tell whether a failure means the system failed the first one, the second one, or both.
Notice also that these requirements describe *what* the feeder does, not *how*. "Dispense a set portion" says nothing about whether it uses an auger, a rotating wheel, or a trapdoor. Requirements define the problem, not the solution.
### Performance requirements
A functional requirement tells you what the system must do. A <span color="red">**`performance requirement`**</span> tells you **how well** it must do it. Performance requirements are also shall statements, but they include a quantitative value: a number that defines the threshold between passing and failing.
<callout color="gray_bg">
	1. The feeder **shall** hold at least 2.0 kg of dry food.
	2. Each dispensed portion **shall** be within ±10% of the set mass.
	3. The feeder **shall** jam no more than once in 100 dispenses, using kibble from 5 to 20 mm.
	4. The feeder **shall** dispense on schedule for at least 48 hours without household power.
</callout>
Each of these can be checked with a test. "Within ±10% of the set mass": dispense 20 portions and weigh them on a kitchen scale. "48 hours without household power": unplug it and watch. A requirement is <span color="red">**`verifiable`**</span> if you can design a test that produces a clear pass or fail result.
"The feeder shall be accurate" is not a performance requirement. It is a wish.
### Should requirements
Not every goal is mandatory. Some capabilities would improve the product, but the design could succeed without them. For these, you use <span color="red">**`should`**</span> instead of shall. Should statements can be functional or performance requirements and are listed after the shall statements.
<callout color="gray_bg">
	1. The feeder **should** send dispense and jam alerts to the owner's phone.
	2. The feeder **should** warn the owner when less than 1 day of food remains.
</callout>
The *should* keeps a goal in the document, visible and trackable, without forcing every proposer to build a more complex product. In an RFP, should requirements also tell proposers where extra effort can earn credit during evaluation. The choice between shall and should is a design decision, not a writing preference.
### Stay solution-neutral
Every requirements document should describe what, not how. In an RFP, this rule matters even more. You are asking other teams to bring ideas you have not thought of, and a requirement that names a solution shuts those ideas out.

## Constraints, Deliverables, and Schedule
Requirements describe what the system must do. <span color="red">**`Constraints`**</span> describe the limits any solution must live within, no matter how it works. They are often the first thing a proposer checks, because a constraint can rule out an idea before any design work begins.
<callout color="gray_bg">
	**Cost:** Estimated bill of materials cost shall not exceed \$30.
	**Food safety:** All parts that touch food shall be made from food-safe materials.
	**Electrical safety:** The feeder shall run on 12 V DC or less. No user-built wiring may carry household voltage.
	**Pet safety:** The feeder shall have no pinch points or moving parts a pet's paw or tongue can reach.
</callout>
The cost constraint is worth noticing. It traces to the company's target retail price of \$79, not to how well the feeder works. Constraints often come from stakeholders who care about something other than whether the product performs.
<span color="red">**`Deliverables`**</span> list exactly what the proposer hands over. Vague deliverables ("a working robot") lead to arguments at the end of a project. Specific deliverables let both sides agree on what "done" means.
<callout color="gray_bg">
	1. A written proposal (see Submission Instructions).
	2. A design review presentation at the Preliminary Design Review.
	3. One working prototype.
	4. A test report showing results against every performance requirement.
	5. A customer quick-start guide, 2 pages or fewer.
</callout>
**Schedule and milestones** give the dates that matter and the review gates where the customer checks progress. A gate is a meeting where work is either approved to continue or sent back.

## Evaluation Criteria
Evaluation criteria tell proposers how their work will be judged. Publishing them is not a courtesy. It is how the customer steers proposals toward what matters most. If the RFP never says that ease of cleaning counts, proposers will spend their effort on extra features instead.
Most RFPs evaluate in two steps.
**Step 1: Compliance.** Does the proposal meet every shall requirement and every constraint? This is pass/fail. A proposal that exceeds the \$30 cost limit or uses household voltage is not compared with the others, no matter how impressive it is.
**Step 2: Comparison.** Compliant proposals are compared against each other using criteria that trace back to the requirements, the ConOps, and the mission need.
<callout color="gray_bg">
	**Evaluation criteria for the Automatic Pet Feeder:**
	1. **Feeding performance:** portion accuracy, jam resistance, and schedule reliability.
	2. **Ease of use:** setup, refilling, cleaning, and how clearly the feeder reports problems.
	3. **Tamper resistance and pet safety:** how well the feeder holds up against a determined pet.
	4. **Production cost:** how far the bill of materials comes in under the cost limit.
	5. **Technical risk:** how likely the team is to deliver on schedule, given their skills and the maturity of the approach.
</callout>
### Questions and amendments
Proposers will find gaps and contradictions. A professional RFP sets a deadline for questions, answers every question in writing, and shares every answer with **all** proposers so no team gets an advantage. If an answer changes a requirement, the customer issues an <span color="red">**`amendment`**</span> to all proposers.

## Putting It Together
An RFP is a requirements document written for strangers. The ConOps, the shall and should statements are what tell a proposer what to build. The mission need, scope, constraints, and evaluation criteria are what tell them why it matters, where the edges are, and how their answer will be judged.
Before you release an RFP, read it as a proposer would and check:
- [ ] The mission need describes a problem, not a solution.
- [ ] Every ConOps scenario generates at least one requirement.
- [ ] Every requirement is a verifiable shall or should statement.
- [ ] No requirement names a specific mechanism, part, or technology unless a constraint truly demands it.
- [ ] Scope says what is out, not just what is in.
- [ ] Constraints, deliverables, and dates are specific.
- [ ] Evaluation criteria trace to the mission need and requirements.
