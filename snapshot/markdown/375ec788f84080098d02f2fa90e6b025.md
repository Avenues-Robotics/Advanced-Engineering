# **Part 1: ConOps and System-Level Requirements**
*Whole team, \~2–3 sessions. Led by team leads.*
Watch the game video and read the FTC game manual together. Produce a shared document with four sections:
1. **Scoring action inventory**
	List every distinct scoring action, point value, and relevant constraints from the manual.
2. **System-level requirements**
	For each scoring action, write a system-level functional **shall** statement describing what the robot must do, and at least one system-level performance **shall** statement describing how well it must do it. These are robot-level requirements, they describe the whole system, not any individual subsystem. Identify any **should** statements representing desirable but non-mandatory capabilities. Flag which requirements are candidates for Key Driving Requirements based on scoring value or design risk.
3. **Concept of Operations (ConOps)**
	Write 2–3 operating scenarios describing how a competitive robot would play the game. Each scenario should be specific enough that a reader could identify which robot functions are active, in what sequence, and under what conditions. Scenarios must cover normal operation, at least one edge case, and the autonomous period as a distinct scenario.
4. **Subsystem decomposition**
	Identify the major subsystems of the robot. For each system-level requirement, assign it to the subsystem responsible for satisfying it. This is an ownership map, not a design. Name and scope each subsystem, but do not write subsystem-level requirements here. That work belongs to the individual owners in Part 2.
*Teacher reviews and validates the shared document before Part 2 begins.*

**Required sections:**
1. **Flowdown statement**
	List the system-level shall statements from Part 1 that this subsystem owns. This is the starting point from which all subsystem requirements are derived.
2. **Operating scenarios**
	Expand the relevant ConOps scenarios from Part 1 for your specific subsystem. Describe each scenario your subsystem must perform in.  Scenarios should be specific enough that a reader could determine what the subsystem must do in each case.
	<callout icon="2️⃣" color="gray_bg">
		**For ME2 (advanced options for ME1)**
		Include edge cases, and failure modes. 
	</callout>
3. **Functional requirements**
	Write shall statements describing what the subsystem must do. One function per statement, traceable to a system-level requirement.
	<callout icon="2️⃣" color="gray_bg">
		For each functional requirement, include a rationale statement explaining why it exists and what assumption or objective it traces to.
	</callout>
4. **Performance requirements**
	Write shall statements with quantitative values specifying how well each function must be performed. 
	<callout icon="2️⃣" color="gray_bg">
		For each performance requirement, include:
		- Rationale - why the value exists and what it traces to
		- Measure of Performance (MOP) -** **how the design will be verified against the shall
		- Technical Performance Measure (TPM) - set a predicted/desired value of each MOP for the final design.
	</callout>
5. **Interface Requirements**
	Write shall statements specifying what your subsystem requires from, or provides to, another subsystem at each physical, electrical, or data boundary between them (e.g., mounting interface, power/signal connection, timing or sequencing dependency). For each interface requirement, confirm it with the teammate who owns the subsystem on the other side of that boundary, note who you coordinated with.
	Ex. "The intake shall deliver artifacts to the shooter's ready position within ±5mm of the shooter's intake point."
6. **Should statements**
	List desirable but non-mandatory goals, clearly distinguished from shall statements.
	<callout icon="2️⃣" color="gray_bg">
		For each, include a rationale statement explaining why it is desirable but not mandatory.
	</callout>
7. **Key Driving Requirements (KDRs)**
	Identify which requirements carry the most design or schedule risk and briefly justify why.

## Rubric
### Requirements
<columns>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Proficient**
		- ConOps covering the basic anticipated operating scenarios from which requirements can be fully derived.
		- Functional requirements stated as "shall" statements describing what the system must do, with one function per statement. Ex. "The arm shall extend to reach the high goal."
		- Separate performance requirements as "shall" statements with a quantitative value specifying how well each function must be performed. Ex. "The arm shall extend to a minimum height of 900 mm."
		- Shall statements specifying what one subsystem of a design requires from, or provides to, another subsystem at each physical, electrical, or data boundary between them (e.g., mounting interface, power/signal connection, timing or sequencing dependency).
		- "Should" statements that identify desirable but non-mandatory goals, distinct from "shall" requirements.
		- Specific enough detail that a peer could determine, after testing, whether each one was met or not.
	</column>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Advanced**
		In addition to Proficient…
		- ConOps covering most anticipated operating scenarios from which requirements can be fully derived.
		- Rationale statements on each requirement, capturing why the requirement exists and what assumption or objective it traces to. Ex. "The arm shall extend to a minimum height of 900 mm. High goal scoring yields 3x the points of low goal; arm reach is a Key Driving Requirement."
		- Measures of Performance (MOPs) derived from performance requirements, the quantitative metrics used to verify the design meets each "shall." Ex. "Measured extension height ≥ 900 mm in 5 of 5 trials."
		- Technical Performance Measures (TPMs), with predicted vs. actual values tracked across build iterations to surface deficiencies before they become failures.
		- A completeness check on the requirement set: every function identified in the ConOps has at least one corresponding "shall" statement.
		- A review of requirements for redundancy, removing or consolidating statements that duplicate one another.
	</column>
</columns>
### Technical Writing
<columns>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Proficient**
		- Written explanations that connect a design decision, test result, or prediction to the underlying engineering reasoning, requirement, or physics, using accurate technical vocabulary.
		- A clear, specific claim about whether a design meets a requirement, supported by evidence such as test data, CAD verification, or calculations.
		- Evidence that is relevant and sufficient to support the claim, including quantitative evidence (measurements, calculations, statistics) where available.
		- Because technical writing is a developing skill, proficient work successfully communicates the claim and evidence, but may contain minor errors in organization, clarity, or vocabulary use.
	</column>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Advanced**
		- Explanations and arguments that are logically coherent, using clear, quantitative reasoning to connect evidence to the claim rather than asserting a conclusion.
		- Quantitative reasoning used to reflect on the strength of the claim, including its limitations, sources of uncertainty, or where more evidence would be needed.
		- Writing that is concise and efficient, communicating complex engineering ideas at a level of depth and technicality appropriate to the intended audience (a teammate, a judge, a general reader).
		- Clear multi-paragraph structure that separates distinct ideas, design intent, evidence, and conclusion, rather than blending them together.
	</column>
</columns>
