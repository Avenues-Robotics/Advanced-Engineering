### Electric Current
**Electric current measures the rate of flow of electrons through a circuit**.  This is usually measured in units of **Amps** (A) or **milliamps** (mA).  Imagine sitting inside of an electrical wire counting the number of electrons that pass you each second.  You would be measuring the current in units of electrons per second.
<columns>
	<column ratio="50">
		For any normal household circuit that number would be enormous.  So instead we use the standard unit **Amps** to measure electrical current.  One Amp is equal to 6,240,000,000,000,000,000 electrons per second.  For circuits with weaker currents you will often see the current written in milliamps or mA.  One milliamp is one thousandth of an Amp.
		Current is related to the amount of electric kinetic energy carried by a circuit.  Higher currents transfer more energy per second than lower currents.  So a high current can make a bulb brighter as each electron transfers a small amount of its energy into radiation.  In your house, appliances that use high currents include electric ovens, refrigerators, air conditioners, and water heaters.

### Electric Potential/Voltage
**Electric potential or voltage measures the force or pressure exerted on the electrons in the circuit.**  This force might be provided by a battery, a generator in a power plant.  Electric potential or voltage is measured in units of **Volts**.  Most small electronic devices use small voltages.  For example, most small devices that run on a USB cable use 5 Volts of electric potential to push the electrons around its circuits.  Electrical outlets in your home or school on the other hand have a voltage of 120 V.  This means that they are capable of pushing electrons much harder.  While your toaster uses that full 120 Volts, most smaller appliances use an adapter to change the voltage from 120 V to a smaller value.

### Electric Charge vs. Battery Charge
Electric charge is the property of subatomic particles that allows them to experience electrical forces. Electrons have negative charges while protons have positive charges.  The net charge or total charge on an object is the difference between the total number of electrons and protons.
However, in the real world when we talk about electric charge we aren’t referring to the net charge on an object.  More commonly, we use charge to describe the state of a battery.  We say, “my phone battery is fully charged.”  This does not mean that the battery is full of excess electrons; this would be very bad for the electronics in your phone!  Instead, when a battery is “charged” it is full of molecules that allow electrons to be separated in a chemical reaction.  When it is “dead” those molecules have been transformed into another kind of molecule.  In the lithium-ion battery shown below, the battery has a full charge when the lithium atoms are on the left side trapped in LiₓC₆ molecules.  When the battery is being used (or discharged) the lithium atom loses an electron to the circuit and then the positive lithium ion (Li⁺) detaches from the LiₓC₆ graphite molecules, moves to the right and grabs a free electron from the positive terminal in order to form lithium cobalt oxide (LiCoO₂) molecules.  The amount of charge that the battery has is related to how many of the lithium (Li) atoms are in graphite (LiₓC₆) form compared to how many are in lithium cobalt oxide (LiCoO₂) form.

The charge of a battery is measured in units of amp hours (Ah) or milliamp hours (mAh).  A typical FTC battery can hold about 3000 mAh, which means that about 60,000,000,000,000,000,000,000 electrons can be freed when the battery as discharged.
