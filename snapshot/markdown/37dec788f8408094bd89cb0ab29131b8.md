# Project 2: Subsystem Conceptual Design
## Overview
*\~2 weeks. Runs 9/22–10/8, overlapping with early build season.*
In Project 1, you defined what your subsystem must do. In Project 2, you decide how it will do it. You will research existing solutions, generate multiple distinct design concepts, compare them systematically based on the requirements, and select a preferred concept.
The output of this project is your subsystem's conceptual design document. It becomes the starting point for your detailed subsystem design, where you will develop the chosen concept into a full detailed design in CAD.
<empty-block/>
## Part 1: Research and Concept Generation
Goal: Research what has been tried before and generate multiple meaningfully distinct design concepts for your subsystem.
**Required sections:**
1. **Research**
	Conduct research and document existing solutions.  Sources might include mechanisms from past FTC and FRC seasons and/or relevant mechanisms from other domains like consumer products or industrial systems. For each source, identify what features are worth borrowing, adapting, or avoiding, and explain why.
	Use the [Prior Art Research Notes Template](https://docs.google.com/document/d/1orMO1n6WR2P0KqW0ehzj7Qc0q0MdB9F0Or37286p1e8/copy) to compile your research notes.
	<empty-block/>
2. **Design concepts**
	Generate at least three meaningfully distinct concepts (not variations on the same idea) that each plausibly satisfy your subsystem requirements. Concepts must differ in their fundamental operating principle, not just in detail.
	For each concept
	1. Produce an annotated sketch identifying key features, how the concept works at a functional level, and how it might interface with adjacent subsystems.  Your sketch can be a photo of a hand drawing in your notebook or CAD mock-ups.  The goal of your sketches is to communicate each design concept clearly enough for a teammate to understand how it functions. Quality sketches include:
		- multiple titled views (ex. top, side, 3D)
		- multiple stages of motion (ex. open, closed, extended, retracted)
		- labels
		- dimensions
	2. Explain the anticipated benefits and limitations of the design concept.  Record what it does well and where it falls short.
	<callout icon="2️⃣" color="gray_bg">
		For ME2 (advanced option for ME1)
		1. Identify risks associated with the design concept including technical risks (can we build this?), performance risks (will it meet the MOP?), and schedule risks (can we build it in time?).
	</callout>
*You will submit Part 1 for feedback and possible revisions before moving on to Part 2.*
## Part 2: Concept Selection
Compare your concepts systematically and select a preferred direction. Every claim in the selection rationale must trace back to your subsystem requirements or team ConOps.
See <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/385ec788f8408087b8cbdd5494b8a000"/> 
**Required sections:**
1. **Evaluation criteria**
	List the criteria you will use to compare concepts. Criteria should trace to a specific requirement or ConOps scenario from your requirements document.  If you find you need to add a criterion that is not in your requirements document, you should carefully consider adding it.  
	<callout icon="2️⃣" color="gray_bg">
		For ME2 (advanced option for ME1)
		Identify which criteria correspond to key driving requirements and justify any weighting decisions quantitatively.
	</callout>
2. **Pugh matrix**
	Compare all concepts against each other using a Pugh matrix, with evaluation criteria as rows and concepts as columns. One concept is designated as the baseline. 
	<callout icon="2️⃣" color="gray_bg">
		For ME2 (advanced option for ME1)
		Explain why that concept was chosen as the baseline.
	</callout>
	*The Pugh Matrix is best done with a team.  The more expertise and experience your team has, the better you will be able to compare your design alternatives.  You should gather a team with as much experience as possible and facilitate a structured discussion to fill out your Pugh matrix.*
3. **Concept selection rationale**
	State your preferred concept and explain why it was chosen using evidence from the requirements, research, and Pugh matrix results. 
	<callout icon="2️⃣" color="gray_bg">
		For ME2 (advanced option for ME1)
		Summarize the identified risks in your choice and how those risks might be managed.
	</callout>
4. **Interface notes**
	Describe how your selected concept will connect to adjacent subsystems. Flag any interface constraints or dependencies that the rest of the team needs to know about.
## Rubric
### Conceptual Design
<columns>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Proficient**
		- Structured research to inform concept generation, documenting what has been tried before (existing robots, relevant mechanisms) and identifying which features are worth borrowing, adapting, or avoiding.
		- At least three meaningfully distinct design concepts (not variations on the same idea), each plausibly satisfying the system requirements.
		- Design concepts documented with an annotated sketch or CAD mock-up identifying key features, showing what the concept looks like and how it works at a functional level.
		- Anticipated benefits and obvious limitations recorded for each concept.
		- All concepts compared against each other using a decision matrix.
		- A preferred concept selected, with the reason for the selection stated.
	</column>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Advanced**
		In addition to Proficient…
		- Each evaluation criterion in the decision matrix traced back to a specific requirement or ConOps scenario, so the selection rationale is grounded in the requirements rather than preference.
		- Design concepts distinguished by identified risks, including technical risks (can we build this?), performance risks (will it meet the MOP?), and schedule risks (can we build it in time?).
		- Rationale for the baseline selection, showing it to be the most credible and well understood concept, not arbitrarily chosen.
		- The final design decision documented with explicit reference to the trade study results, identified risks, and requirements.
	</column>
</columns>
### Technical Writing
<columns>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Proficient**
		- Written explanations that connect a design decision, test result, or prediction to the underlying engineering reasoning, requirement, or physics, using accurate technical vocabulary.
		- A clear, specific claim about whether a design meets a requirement, supported by evidence such as test data, CAD verification, or calculations.
		- Evidence that is relevant and sufficient to support the claim, including quantitative evidence (measurements, calculations, statistics) where available.
		- Because technical writing is a developing skill, proficient work successfully communicates the claim and evidence, but may contain minor errors in organization, clarity, or vocabulary use.
	</column>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Advanced**
		- Explanations and arguments that are logically coherent, using clear, quantitative reasoning to connect evidence to the claim rather than asserting a conclusion.
		- Quantitative reasoning used to reflect on the strength of the claim, including its limitations, sources of uncertainty, or where more evidence would be needed.
		- Writing that is concise and efficient, communicating complex engineering ideas at a level of depth and technicality appropriate to the intended audience (a teammate, a judge, a general reader).
		- Clear multi-paragraph structure that separates distinct ideas, design intent, evidence, and conclusion, rather than blending them together.
	</column>
</columns>
