## Introduction
A PID controller is only as effective as the values of the gains ($`K_p, K_i, K_d, \text{ and } K_f`$). <span color="red">**`Tuning`**</span> is the process used to measure the value of each gain to optimize a mechanism’s performance.  Novices often treat tuning as a trial and error process: adjust a number, watch the mechanism, and adjust again. This approach is slow, rarely converges on good values, and leaves no record of what was learned. Effective tuning is an engineering process built on careful planning, measurement, and data collection. Each change to a gain is a controlled experiment: one variable is changed, the response is measured under identical conditions, and the result is recorded and compared against criteria defined in advance.
## Planning the Tuning Process
Tuning begins before the mechanism moves. The first task is to define acceptable performance in measurable terms. A controller is usually evaluated by its response to a <span color="red">**`step`**</span>, a sudden change in target, such as commanding a slide to move from its resting position to a point 1000 encoder ticks higher. Four characteristics of that response are commonly measured:
- <span color="red">**`rise time`**</span>: the time required for the mechanism to first reach the target after the target changes.
- <span color="red">**`overshoot`**</span>: the maximum distance the mechanism travels past the target, often expressed as a percentage of the step size.
- <span color="red">**`settling time`**</span>: the time required for the mechanism to come to rest within an acceptable band around the target, for example within 2% of the step size.
- <span color="red">**`steady-state error`**</span>: the error that remains once the mechanism has settled.
These criteria function as performance requirements for the controller. For example: *The slide shall reach a target 1000 ticks above its starting position in under 0.8 seconds, with overshoot below 5% and steady-state error below 10 ticks.* Writing the requirements before testing establishes when tuning is finished; without them, tuning has no defined endpoint.
The characteristics also compete with one another. A controller tuned for the shortest possible rise time typically overshoots more, and a controller tuned to eliminate overshoot typically responds more slowly. The requirements determine which compromise is appropriate. A lift that must place a payload precisely may tolerate a slower response, while a drivetrain in a timed autonomous routine may accept modest overshoot in exchange for speed.
The second planning task is to design a repeatable test. Every trial should use the same starting position, the same step size, the same load, and a battery within a narrow voltage range. For mechanisms affected by gravity, the test should include steps in both directions, since gravity assists motion one way and opposes it the other.
## Measurement and Data Collection
Visual observation is not sufficient for tuning. An overshoot of a few millimeters, or an oscillation lasting a fraction of a second, is difficult to see and impossible to quantify by eye. On every pass through the loop, the controller should record the time, the target, the measurement, and the output. Recording the P, I, D, and F contributions separately is also valuable, because it reveals which term dominates the output at each moment. These values can be displayed through telemetry and graphed on a dashboard. A graph of the measurement versus time, plotted together with the target, is the primary tool for evaluating a response.

Three rules keep the data meaningful:
1. **Change one gain per trial.** If two gains change at once, the cause of any difference in the response cannot be determined.
2. **Repeat each trial.** A single run can be misleading. Running each set of gains several times (three is a reasonable minimum) reveals how much the response varies on its own.
3. **Record every trial, including failures.** A trial that oscillates or overshoots badly identifies the boundary of acceptable gains, which is as useful as a trial that succeeds.
<callout icon="⚠️" color="gray_bg">
	**Before the first trial.** Confirm that a positive output moves the mechanism in the direction that reduces a positive error. If the direction is reversed, the controller drives the mechanism away from the target with increasing power. Begin with small gains, limit the maximum output, keep the mechanism clear of hard stops, and be prepared to stop the program immediately.
</callout>
## Step 1: Tune the Feedforward Term (F)
Tuning proceeds in the order F, P, I, D. Feedforward is tuned first because it addresses the predictable portion of the load. If P were tuned while gravity remained uncompensated, the proportional term would be doing two jobs at once, and the resulting gain would no longer be correct once feedforward was added.
For a linear slide with a constant $`K_f`$:
1. Set $`K_p`$, $`K_i`$, and $`K_d`$ to zero.
2. Position the slide near the middle of its travel.
3. Increase $`K_f`$ in small increments (0.01, for example) until the slide no longer descends. Record this value as the lower bound.
4. Continue increasing $`K_f`$ until the slide begins to rise. Record this value as the upper bound.
5. Set $`K_f`$ to the midpoint of the two bounds.
The two bounds exist because static friction holds the slide in place over a range of output values. The midpoint is the best estimate of the output that exactly balances gravity. Repeating the measurement at several heights confirms whether a constant $`K_f`$ is an appropriate model; if the bounds change with height, as they may for a multi-stage slide, a different model is required. Arms and velocity-controlled mechanisms may require a more complex feedforward term and measurements at multiple positions, but the approach is the same: measure the output the mechanism requires under known conditions, and fit the model to those measurements.
Feedforward is correctly tuned when, with all feedback gains at zero, the mechanism holds its position wherever it is placed.

## Step 2: Tune the Proportional Term (P)
Because $`K_p`$ converts error, measured in the sensor's units, into motor power, its appropriate size depends on those units. A reasonable starting value produces full power only at a very large error, such as an error equal to the mechanism's full range of travel. For a slide that travels 2000 ticks, a starting value of $`K_p = 1 / 2000 = 0.0005`$ is appropriate.  At this value an error of 2000 gives a power of  $`0.0005 \times 2000 = 1.0`$.
```java
Kp = 0.0005;
P = error * Kp; // for error = 2000, P = 1
motor.setPower(P); 
```
1. Run the step test with the starting $`K_p`$. Record and graph the response.
2. Double $`K_p`$ and repeat. Each increase should shorten the rise time.
3. Continue doubling until overshoot exceeds the requirement or sustained oscillation appears.
4. Refine with smaller changes between the last two values, and select the largest $`K_p`$ that satisfies the overshoot requirement.
Doubling allows a wide range of gains to be explored in a few trials. At the end of this step, the mechanism should approach the target quickly with modest overshoot. A small steady-state error may remain.
![](media/12d8d0e4b2c3b8cc.png)
## Step 3: Tune the Integral Term (I)
The integral term is added only if steady-state error exceeds the requirement. When feedforward has been tuned well, it is often unnecessary, because feedforward has already canceled the persistent force that causes most steady-state error.
1. Begin with a $`K_i`$ much smaller than $`K_p`$. One common starting point is one-tenth of $`K_p`$.
2. Increase $`K_i`$ gradually, comparing steady-state error and settling time across trials.
3. Select the smallest $`K_i`$ that brings steady-state error within the requirement.
An excessive $`K_i`$ produces additional overshoot followed by a slow, rolling oscillation, because the accumulated error must unwind before the mechanism can settle. Logging a graph of the I term (`I = integralSum * Ki`) is particularly useful here: if it continues to grow while the mechanism is not moving, the controller is experiencing integral windup, and the remedies described in the Advanced PID callout of <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3f7ec788f84081d0a468e7be52d79e62"/> should be applied.
![](media/28a56842bb8a8cce.png)
## Step 4: Tune the Derivative Term (D)
The derivative term is added only if overshoot or oscillation exceeds the requirement.
1. Begin with a small $`K_d`$.
2. Increase $`K_d`$ gradually until overshoot falls within the requirement.
3. Watch for signs of an excessive $`K_d`$: a sluggish approach to the target, an audible buzz or jitter in the motor, or an output graph that appears noisy. These symptoms indicate that the derivative term is amplifying sensor noise.
Once the derivative term is damping the motion, $`K_p`$ can often be raised further to shorten the rise time. A few additional trials returning to Step 2, with $`K_d`$ held fixed, may yield a faster response that still meets every requirement. Tuning is therefore iterative rather than strictly sequential.
![](media/fa5c945991d588e8.png)
## Verifying the Final Gains
A set of gains that performs well in one test has not yet been verified. The final gains should be tested across the range of conditions the mechanism will encounter: repeated trials, steps in both directions, small and large step sizes, a range of battery voltages, and any loads the mechanism will carry. Each result is compared against the requirements defined during planning.
The final gains are recorded in the tuning log along with the date and the configuration of the mechanism. Gains are specific to a particular mechanism; a change in mass, gear ratio, motor, or sensor changes the system's response, and the controller must be re-tuned. The tuning log is a record of the engineering process: it documents not only which gains were chosen but also the evidence that justifies them.
