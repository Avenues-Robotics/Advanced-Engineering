### Overview
In Project 2, you selected a design concept for your subsystem. In Project 3, you will develop that concept into a complete, verified CAD design ready for fabrication. This is also where your design decisions get checked: before you build, you'll verify that your CAD model actually satisfies the requirements you wrote in Project 1.
<callout icon="🏅" color="gray_bg">
	**For FTC Competitors**
	Your robot will keep changing throughout the season, that's normal for FTC which pushes a very aggressive timeline which is fun, but does not always allow for thoughtful engineering. Treat the first competition as a review of the first version, not the final design.  **Your detailed design CAD model and other documents for this project should reflect your subsystem as it exists** for your team’s first competition.
</callout>
### Part 0: Onshape Tutorials
The CAD Model in this project will be built in Onshape, a professional-level cloud-based CAD program that allows multiple teammates to access documents simultaneously and uses version control to track your design’s revision history.  Before you begin modeling your sub-system, you need to have basic-proficiency in Onshape’s part modeling and assembly tools.  The tutorials here will help you build your skills.

### Part 1: CAD Model
Goal: build a complete CAD model of your selected concept that communicates your design intent and can be checked against your requirements.
1. **Subsystem CAD model**<br>Model your selected Project 2 concept in Onshape. Produce a complete, well-organized model with all major components modeled included, part and assembly names should make the model easy to navigate and understand for a teammate or coach, assemblies should be mated to reflect how the parts will move relative to each other, and the design.
2. **Bill of materials**<br>List part names, quantities, and materials for your subsystem. 
<callout icon="2️⃣" color="gray_bg">
	**For ME2 (advanced options for ME1)**
	Advanced models should be fully parametric where design intent depends on dimensional relationships and key dimensions are named variables so changes propagate through the model rather than requiring manual updates.
	1. **Version Control<br>**Use Onshape’s version control system to create clearly named branches for testing potential options, merging to the main branch when appropriate.
	2. **Drawings**<br>Produce dimensioned drawings of your assembly and key manufactured parts.  Drawings should include a least three orthographic views (front, top, side, etc.) with key dimensions and an isometric (3D) view. ### Part 2: Requirements Verification
Goal: check your CAD design against your Project 1 requirements before fabrication — don't wait for a physical failure to discover a design gap.
1. **Verification checklist**<br>List each functional and performance requirement from your Project 1 document, determine whether your CAD model as designed satisfies it, does not satisfy it, or cannot yet be determined from CAD alone.
<callout icon="2️⃣" color="gray_bg">
	**For ME2 (advanced options for ME1)**
	1. **Verification table**<br>For each "shall" requirement, document the verification method (analysis, inspection of CAD, or similarity to a previously tested design), the supporting evidence, and a pass/fail/TBD judgment.
	2. **Verification closure**<br>Every "shall" requirement should end with a pass or a *justified* TBD — not an open question. For each TBD, write a risk statement and a plan for closing it (what test or build artifact will resolve it, and when).
</callout>
### Part 3: Physics in Your Design
Goal: recognize where physics can inform your design decisions. Every subsystem has questions that physics can answer before you build, such as whether a motor is strong enough or how fast a mechanism will move. In ME1 you are not required to complete the analysis yet, but you do need to find a real physics question in your design and think it through. You are welcome to start the analysis now. You will have opportunities to finish it later in the course.
1. **Physics question**<br>Identify at least one question about your subsystem that physics could answer. For example: *Can this motor lift the arm while it holds a game element? How fast will the slide extend? Will this bracket bend under load?* 
	For your question, explain:
	- How does the design of the subsystem depends on the answer to this question?
	- What physical quantities are involved with your question; which ones you already know and which would you need to measure?
<callout icon="2️⃣" color="gray_bg">
	**Mechanics for ME2 (not required for ME1 students)**
	**Physics-based design justification \[Mechanics outcome, separate from Detailed Design\]**<br>Perform a physics analysis that answers your question and justifies at least one key design decision in your subsystem. Examples might include required motor torque to lift or move a known load, gear ratio selection for a required speed or torque, structural member sizing, or the linear speed of a mechanism. Show your work with units and stated assumptions, and connect the result back to a specific requirement or design choice.
	**Advanced analyses**
	- are multi-step and involve a connected chain of interactions where the output of one calculation becomes the input of the next.
	- are used to identify and quantify structural safety factors and design margins for the subsystem’s performance.   Examples might include:
		- safety factor comparison for different materials that were considered (aluminum vs. steel)
		- design margin between expected mechanisms speed and the speed listed in the design requirements.
	- Are presented as typed mathematical text using an equation editor like MathType or learn a little LaTex and use a Docs extension like Auto-LaTeX Equations. 
</callout>

### Reflection
Write a few paragraphs (roughly 300–500 words) in your reflection document reflecting on your Project 3 process. Include images or references to specific entries from your engineering notebook as evidence for your claims, don't just summarize from memory.
At minimum reflect on the following:
1. **What changed, and why.** Compare your final CAD model to your Project 2 concept. What's different, and what specific piece of information (a fit problem, a part that didn't exist, feedback from a teammate, a physics calculation) caused each change?
2. **What your verification checklist actually found.** Did checking your CAD against your Project 1 requirements reveal anything you didn't expect, a requirement that turned out to be unrealistic, unverifiable, or already violated by your design? How did you handle it?
3. **Next Steps.** Your subsystem almost certainly isn't "done" at this point, that’s expected. What would you change if you were going to continue iterating on this design? Why didn't you make that change for this project (time, information, priority)?
<callout icon="2️⃣" color="gray_bg">
	**ME 2 Students**
	**Where the math changed your mind.** Did your physics analysis confirm a decision you'd already made, or did it change your design? How does “doing the math” change your thinking about design in general?
</callout>

### Rubric
#### Detailed Design
<columns>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Proficient**
		- A complete and well organized CAD model with all major components modeled or represented, assemblies mated correctly, and the design visually inspectable from any angle.
		- The CAD document organized with sketches dimensioned, each part modeled and kept separate from other parts where appropriate, and assemblies built distinctly from the individual part files.
		- A bill of materials (BOM) listing part names, quantities, and materials for the subsystem.
		- The CAD model checked against each functional requirement using a structured checklist: for each requirement, whether the design as modeled satisfies it, does not satisfy it, or cannot yet be determined.
	</column>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Advanced**
		In addition to Proficient…
		- BOM entries that fully specify key structural members and purchased components (motors, servos, bearings, fasteners), precisely enough that a teammate could order or fabricate them without further information.
		- A pre-fabrication verification table, listing each "shall" requirement with the verification method (analysis, inspection of CAD, or similarity to tested design), the evidence from the CAD or calculation, and a pass/fail/TBD judgment.
		- Requirements marked TBD identified, with documentation of what physical test or build artifact will be needed to close them.
	</column>
</columns>
#### Technical Communication
<columns>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Proficient**
		- Written explanations that connect a design decision, test result, or prediction to the underlying engineering reasoning, requirement, or physics, using accurate technical vocabulary.
		- A clear, specific claim about whether a design meets a requirement, supported by evidence such as test data, CAD verification, or calculations.
		- Evidence that is relevant and sufficient to support the claim, including quantitative evidence (measurements, calculations, statistics) where available.
		- Because technical writing is a developing skill, proficient work successfully communicates the claim and evidence, but may contain minor errors in organization, clarity, or vocabulary use.
	</column>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Advanced**
		- Explanations and arguments that are logically coherent, using clear, quantitative reasoning to connect evidence to the claim rather than asserting a conclusion.
		- Quantitative reasoning used to reflect on the strength of the claim, including its limitations, sources of uncertainty, or where more evidence would be needed.
		- Writing that is concise and efficient, communicating complex engineering ideas at a level of depth and technicality appropriate to the intended audience (a teammate, a judge, a general reader).
		- Clear multi-paragraph structure that separates distinct ideas, design intent, evidence, and conclusion, rather than blending them together.
	</column>
</columns>
#### Mechanics (Optional for ME1)
<columns>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Proficient**
		- At least one physics analysis directly tied to a design decision with work shown in units and assumptions stated explicitly.
			- Examples: required motor torque to lift or move a known load, gear ratio selection, structural member sizing; joint reach to satisfy a height or extension requirement, range of motion analysis.
		- Concepts at this level: force (N), torque (N·m), mass (kg), weight (N), distance / displacement (m), angle (degrees or radians), gear ratio (unitless), mechanical advantage (unitless), velocity (m/s), and rotational speed (RPM or rad/s).
	</column>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Advanced**
		In addition to Proficient…
		- A connected chain of physics analysis covering the primary load path or control loop for a subsystem, not just one calculation, but a sequence where the output of one step is the input to the next.
			- Examples: motor torque to gear reduction to output force to factor of safety on structure; input motion to linkage geometry to end effector position and velocity.
		- A factor of safety calculated for at least one critical element: the worst-case load identified, the rated capacity of the material or component determined, the ratio computed, and an explicit judgment of whether the margin is acceptable given the consequence of failure.
		- Concepts at this level (builds on Level 1): acceleration (m/s²), angular acceleration (rad/s²), moment of inertia (kg·m²), normal force (N), friction force (N), coefficient of friction (unitless), tension (N), compression (N), shear force (N), bending moment (N·m), stress (Pa), factor of safety (unitless), rated load / capacity (N or N·m), work (J), and power (W).
	</column>
</columns>
