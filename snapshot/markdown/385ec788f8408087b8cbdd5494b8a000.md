# Why Systematic Decision Making Matters
By the time you reach conceptual design, you've done real work. You've anyalyzed the problem, written requirements, researched prior art, and generated multiple distinct design concepts. Each concept represents a direction your subsystem could go. Now comes a decision that will shape everything downstream: which one do you build?
The temptation is to go with your gut: pick the concept that excites you most, or the one used by a competitor, or the one your most confident teammate is pushing for. The problem isn't that intuition is always wrong. The problem is that you can't defend it, and you can't improve it.
When a design decision is made informally, a few things tend to happen. Requirements get ignored in favor of what seems cool or buildable. The loudest voice in the room wins. Weeks later, when the design runs into trouble, no one can reconstruct why the team made the choices it did which makes it nearly impossible to course-correct intelligently.
Systematic concept selection doesn't replace your judgment. It structures it. By making your criteria explicit, comparing concepts against those criteria one at a time, and recording your reasoning, you create a selection process that is transparent, revisable, and grounded in your actual requirements. If your concept is the right choice, the matrix will show it. If it isn't, you want to know now, not after you've machined parts and burned three weeks of build season.
The Pugh Matrix is the standard tool for this at the conceptual design stage. It is used in aerospace, automotive, consumer product development, and anywhere else engineers need to make defensible decisions between multiple viable alternatives. What follows is a guide to how it works and how to use it well.
# The Pugh Matrix
The Pugh Matrix (PM) is a type of matrix diagram that allows for the comparison of a number of design candidates, leading ultimately to a selection of which best meets a set of criteria. It also permits a degree of qualitative optimization of the alternative concepts through the generation of hybrid candidates.
The Pugh Matrix is easy to use and relies upon a series of pairwise comparisons between design candidates against a number of criteria or requirements. One of its key advantages over other decision-making tools is its ability to handle a large number of decision criteria.
# Why use a Pugh Matrix?
Many decisions concern a number of interwoven factors or criteria that humans struggle to handle, resulting in inconsistent and irrational decisions. The Pugh Matrix provides a simple approach to taking these multiple factors into account when reaching a decision. By exploiting people's innate ability to make a direct comparison between two alternatives, it allows for subjective opinions about one alternative versus another to be made more objective. The Pugh Matrix also allows for simple sensitivity analysis to be performed, thereby providing some information as to the robustness of a particular decision.

The matrix shows four candidate design concepts (A, B, C, D) evaluated against 10 criteria. In constructing a Pugh Matrix, one design concept — in this example Design Concept A — is selected as the **baseline** (or **datum**). This baseline is scored as "0" against all criteria. The other candidate design concepts are then compared against the baseline for each criterion:
- Better than the baseline → **+**
- Worse than the baseline → **–**
- The same as the baseline → **0**
The overall evaluation is made by summing the "+" and "–" scores for each concept.
The Pugh Matrix can also be used to perform qualitative optimization by combining candidate concepts into **hybrid candidates**. Figure 1 shows two such hybrids: Concept BC and Concept BD.
## The Pugh Process
The process for constructing a Pugh Matrix comprises five steps. This assumes that alternative candidate design options have already been generated.
**Step 1: Identify and clearly define the criteria for selection.**
Typically, the design requirements can be used as criteria, either in part or in whole. The criteria should reflect the requirements laid out in the team’s requirements document. The robustness and validity of the outcome is fundamentally dependent on an appropriate set of criteria. Rushing this step usually results in a non-robust outcome that is challenged and overturned.
Often, in the process of selecting criteria to compare design alternatives, the team will uncover shall or should requirements that were not stated in the original requirements. When this happens the team should carefully consider whether those requirements should be added or if the criteria being considered should be rejected.
**Step 2: Select one candidate design option as the baseline and score all criteria as "0" for this baseline.**
A good choice for baseline is the most credible or well-understood concept, the one the team considers most likely to work, or the one that most closely resembles an existing solution you've already researched. Its performance against each criterion should be reasonably predictable, which gives you a stable reference point. If your baseline is uncertain or poorly understood, your comparisons inherit that uncertainty.
A reasonable baseline is often the most conventional approach to a system's function: the mechanism type you've seen most often in prior art, or the one you feel most confident you could build. You don't have to think the baseline is the best concept, just that you understand it well enough to say whether other concepts are better or worse at each criterion.
**Step 3: Compare each candidate design option against the baseline, criteria by criteria, and assign a pairwise score.**
- 0 = same as baseline
- + = better than baseline
- − = worse than baseline
It is also possible to add extra levels of discrimination:
- ++ = much better
- −− = much worse
**Step 4: Calculate the total score for each candidate by summing the "+" and "−" counts. **
The highest-ranked concept is a candidate for selection — but use common sense. Do not simply select the highest-ranked concept without examining why.
**Step 5: Consider hybrid concepts by combining the best features of each alternative. This is a form of qualitative optimization.** 
For example, if Concept A scores well on compactness but poorly on cycle speed, and Concept B is the reverse, a hybrid that borrows A's geometry and B's actuation mechanism may outperform either original.  After thinking through the hybrid design, compare it to the baseline just like the other design candidates.
**Step 6: Make the decision and record the reasons behind it.**
If the matrix produces a clear winner, resist the urge to immediately move on. Take a moment to ask: does this result feel right? If the answer is yes, document why. Record the key criteria that drove the outcome and any risks associated with the chosen concept. If the answer is no, treat that instinct as a signal worth investigating. Either your criteria don't fully capture what matters, your scores contain an error, or there's a constraint the matrix didn't account for. In either case, the goal is to leave Step 6 with a decision you can explain and defend, not just one the numbers produced.
Quite often there is no clear "winner," but there is usually a clear "loser." In such cases, perform a sanity check (does the decision make sense?) and remove the losing option. At this point the criteria can be **weighted** to give better differentiation — typically on a 1–5 scale.  The weighing process is explained in the example below.  If there is still no clear winner, the matrix is indicating that there is insufficient information to discriminate between the options. In such cases it will be necessary to:
- Refine the criteria
- Perform more work to gather information that distinguishes the options
- Use an alternative or supplementary selection approach

- 4-slot Electric Toaster
- Toasting Fork and Fire

**Step 1** is to determine the selection criteria. Your criteria should come directly from your  requirements document, be not invented fresh for the matrix. Shall statements are your primary criteria: these are the non-negotiable functional and performance requirements your concept must satisfy. Should statements make good secondary criteria: desirable goals that differentiate concepts when the shall statements don't produce a clear winner.
A criterion for each shall and should statement keeps the matrix grounded in your requirements rather than in opinion. If you find yourself wanting to add a criterion that doesn't trace back to any requirement, stop and ask whether it should have been a requirement in the first place.

**Step 4** adds the total +s and –s and calculates the total score for each design. With a total score of 0, the 4-slot electric toaster is the winner. The electric conveyor is a close second at -2, and the gas grill third at -4. The toasting fork and fire is in a distinct last place at -8.
At this point it is worth examining why one concept is better than another, and performing a sanity check: does the outcome make sense? Quite often it does not, which can indicate that the criteria are incomplete or incorrectly defined. In this case, debate during the evaluation raised an additional criterion: "the toaster should be automatic." Including it further penalized the fire-and-fork option without changing the overall ranking — but made the case for eliminating it clearer.
The remaining three options are all relatively close in total score. To help discriminate between the them, the criteria can be weighted. Not all criteria carry equal importance. A criterion tied to a Key Driving Requirement, one that significantly affects performance or carries high design risk, deserves more weight than one tied to a should statement or a minor functional requirement. Weighting the criteria provides extra discrimination and a form of robustness assessment — does the ranking of options change when weights are applied? In this case, the weighted score makes the gas grill a clear loser, but the two remaining electric options remain very close. This is a typical Pugh Matrix outcome: it reliably eliminates weaker options but often cannot cleanly identify a winner between the top candidates. When this happens, the matrix is indicating that there is insufficient information to decide — not that the method has failed.
In a case like this with two concepts separated by just a few weighted points, the right move is not to flip a coin or defer to whoever argues loudest. A close result is the matrix telling you that your current information isn't sufficient to choose. A few productive next steps:
**Re-examine the criteria that produced the closest scores.** If criteria 13 (Attractive) and 15 (Good Size) are dragging the conveyor's score down, ask whether those scores are accurate. Is the conveyor actually less attractive, or did the team just assume so? Scores based on assumptions rather than evidence are worth revisiting.
**Check the weights.** A weight of 5 on Attractive may not hold up to scrutiny — it's hard to justify ranking aesthetics as critical for a kitchen appliance that will sit on a counter. If any weight feels arbitrary in hindsight, revise it before declaring a winner.
**Consider what additional information would break the tie.** Could you build a quick prototype or mock-up of the conveyor to actually compare it against the 4-slot toaster on the criteria where they're close? Is there a test, sketch, or simulation that would give you more confidence in the scores?
The matrix has done its job — it eliminated the weak options and identified what's genuinely in contention. Resolving the final choice is an engineering judgment call, made with better information than you started with.

# Limitations of the Pugh Matrix
**Incorrect, incomplete, or inadequate selection criteria.**
The quality of the decision is fundamentally related to the quality of the selection criteria. Common problems include:
- *Incorrect criteria* — the team uses their own opinions rather than grounding criteria in requirements. Criteria should be traceable to stakeholder needs or documented requirements.
- *Incomplete criteria* — not all relevant stakeholders or considerations are represented. Technical risk, buildability, and schedule feasibility are commonly overlooked.
- *Inadequate criteria* — poorly defined criteria that can have multiple interpretations (e.g. "low cost" without specifying whether this means purchase cost, manufacturing cost, or development cost) undermine consistent scoring.
**Low granularity of the pairwise scale.**
The simple +/0/– scale is quick to apply but can give poor decision robustness. It is possible to get a different ranking by changing the baseline. Using ++/+/0/–/–– reduces this sensitivity somewhat. Performing robustness checks — changing the baseline or flexing weightings — helps validate the outcome.
**Wrong expertise or insufficient experience.**
The Pugh Matrix extracts knowledge and judgment from the people doing the evaluation. The right result depends on having the right people in the room, not just on following the process correctly.
# Best Practices for Pugh Matrix evaluation
The following practices support a high-quality Pugh Matrix evaluation:
- Team size between 4 and 8
- Team includes members with relevant expertise and experience in the system being evaluated
- Use an experienced, independent facilitator where possible
- Plan for 1-2 hours of focused effort
- Define clearly what decision is being made before starting
- Have validated and clearly defined selection criteria available before the session
- Spend time ensuring the team has a shared understanding of each criterion — document agreed definitions
- Perform robustness checks by flexing weightings or re-scoring borderline pairwise comparisons
- Document the debate and reasoning behind key decisions, not just the scores
