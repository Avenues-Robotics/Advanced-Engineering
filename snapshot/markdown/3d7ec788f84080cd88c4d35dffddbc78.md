## Why Reflection?
Reflection and self-evaluation is an essential part of the learning process.  Students who reflect on where they were, where they are, and where they want to go next set better goals, manage their time better, and feel more empowered to take control of their own learning.  The reflection process is especially important in a flexible, project-based, student-led course like the Advanced Robotics courses where you will have a lot of autonomy in choosing what you want to learn, and when you want to learn it.  One of the goals of this course is to provide the structure to help you become a more reflective student and build reflective writing, goal setting, and time management skills.
## Term Reflections
At the beginning of the course, and at the end of each term, you will write a longer reflection.  The purpose of these reflections is to connect your big picture goals to your work in the course.  These reflections help us (you and Mr. Carpenter) understand your growth over the course of the year and think about how your long-term project work aligns with your goals.
	### Initial Reflection
	What are your goals?  What skills and knowledge are you bringing to the course?  What values do you bring to the course?  What are your strengths?  Where do you need support?
	### End-of-Term Reflections
	What have you learned so far?  What are you proud of?  What needs to change moving forward?
	### End-of-year Reflection
	What did you accomplish?  What are you proud of?  Which goals did you not accomplish but wish you had?  What did you struggle with?  What life lessons have you learned?
## Bi-weekly Check-ins
Every two weeks, the day before our one-on-one meetings, you will will submit a shorter reflection about what you have learned and accomplished over the past two weeks, your goals for the upcoming two weeks and what support you might need to achieve your goals.  These short-term reflections will help me help you stay on track and will also provide a record of your thinking and learning that you can use for your end-of-term and end-of-year reflections.

## Engineering Notebook
In this course, you will make use of an Engineering Notebook. This is a dedicated notebook that you use to document everything you do in robotics. Key points from the readings that you want to be sure to remember, notes from team meetings, brainstorming ideas, diagramming complex ideas, project planning, project documentation, and more.  Your notebook will be the source material for your term reflections and bi-weekly check-ins.  Keeping up with your notebook will make the reflection assignments much easier to complete.
You should aim to write at least one page in your notebook each day you come to robotics. There will be more on days that you are doing more planning/thinking work and on days when you are working intensely.  While I won't be collecting and reading your notebook entries, I may ask if I can look at the contents when reviewing work in class (I will **always** ask first, and respect your choice to refuse).  I am very happy to provide feedback on your approach to your notebook, though a lot of these are personal choices.
Choose a notebook format that lets you do more than just write words. Good engineering notebooks let you work in a variety of modalities, including diagramming, side comments, quotes. I usually prefer **physical written notebooks** because I can easily diagram, draw, remind myself of photos I've taken. They also cut back on digital distractions, and don't need to be charged. And there's a lot of research showing that handwritten notes can have significant cognitive benefits over digital notes! Finally, some find it more satisfying to re-read and store physical books than digital text.
Though I strongly recommend using a physical journal, you are welcome to choose the format that best works for you. Most important of all is that you pick a journal that you can consistently access. Bring your journal to class **every day**. Everything you do for this class should end up in it!

## Quality Reflection: The 5Rs
Not all reflection is equally useful. It's easy to write a page that summarizes what happened without actually learning anything from it. One helpful framework for reflective writing, developed by Bain and colleagues, breaks reflection into five moves that build on each other:
	**Report** — What happened? Describe the situation factually: what you did, who was involved, what the key events were. ("I tried... I noticed... I built...")
	**Respond** — How did you react? Add your honest thoughts, feelings, and observations. ("I felt... I thought...")
	**Relate** — How does this connect to what you already know? Link the experience to past work, prior knowledge, or something similar you've run into before. ("This reminds me of... This is similar to...")
	**Reason** — Why did this happen, and why does it matter? Dig into the causes and the most important factors at play. ("I understand that... The reason this happened was...")
	**Reconstruct** — What will you do differently? Draw a concrete conclusion about your thinking or approach going forward. ("I will now... Next time I'll...")
A check-in or reflection that only Reports and Responds isn't wrong, it's a reasonable starting point, but Relating, Reasoning, and Reconstructing are what turn a notebook entry or reflection into learning you can actually use later. That's the difference between the Proficient and Advanced levels on the Documentation Rubric below.
[More about the 5R Framework](https://reflection.ed.ac.uk/reflectors-toolkit/reflecting-on-experience/5r-framework)
## Documentation Rubric
This is the rubric for your Bi-weekly Check-ins, Term Reflections, Project Reflections and  your contributions to your team’s shared documentation (CAD comments, commit messages, a build log). 
<columns>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Proficient**
		- Reflections reports and respond with specificity and detail: a concrete account of what happened (dates, numbers, events) and an honest response to it, what you thought, felt, or noticed, not a vague summary of either.
		- Check-ins and reflections reference Engineering Notebook entries recording what was done, what changed, and what prompted it (a design decision, a test result, an observation, a measurement, or a peer suggestion), and what comes next.
		- Reflection on each major project: what went well, what didn't, and what you would do differently.
		- Personal learning reflections that identify specific skills or concepts you are actively working to improve.
		<empty-block/>
	</column>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Advanced**
		In addition to Proficient…
		- Reflections relate, reason, and reconstruct: connect what happened to past experience or knowledge, reason about why it mattered or what caused it, and reconstructs your thinking or approach going forward.
		- Check-ins and reflections reference Engineering Notebook entries that include not just what changed and why, but which alternative fixes were considered and why the chosen approach was selected over others, connected to a specific requirement, MOP, or ConOps scenario where applicable.
		- When a design is modified in response to a test finding, an explicit diagnosis of whether the finding indicated a design problem, a build quality problem, or a requirements problem, recorded before the fix is described.
		- Documentation of project work is maintained in a shared digital project management tool (e.g. GitHub Projects) so all team members have visibility into the current state and history of each subsystem.
		- Project reflections that go beyond process, including an honest assessment of the quality of the engineering decisions, not just what happened, but whether they were good choices and why or why not.
		- Personal learning reflections that identify specific skills or concepts you are actively working to improve, with progress on those goals tracked across entries as a narrative of growth rather than isolated observations.
		- Applies change control: before making a significant design change, documents the proposed change, the rationale, and the expected impact on requirements and interfaces, and records the decision and who made it.
	</column>
