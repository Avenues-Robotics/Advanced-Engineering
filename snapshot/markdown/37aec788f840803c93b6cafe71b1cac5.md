# Engineering Requirements
---
Before you build anything (a toy, a bridge, a web application, or a robot) you need to be able to answer two questions clearly: *What does this system have to do?* and *How will we know if it worked?* A requirements document is an engineer’s way of answering those questions. 
Are requirements documents absolutely necessary for a high school engineering project? Will having them make or break your robotics competition season? No. Teams win FTC championships with nothing more than a whiteboard sketch and a good driver. But as projects become bigger and more complex, rigorous, validated requirements documents become absolutely necessary.  Imagine planning a rover mission to Mars with a few whiteboard sketches! When engineering projects take years and thousands of people to execute and the difference between success and failure of a multi-billion dollar mission depends on one electrical connection or the strength of one bolt, the cost of failure is too high to leave to chance.  The engineering profession has developed formal requirements processes to ensure complex projects produce reliable and safe results.
<callout color="gray_bg">
	Check out NASA’s manual for establishing requirements for a real world example of the process described below: [NASA Systems Engineering Handbook](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2018/09/nasa_systems_engineering_handbook_0.pdf) (specifically chapter 4.2).
	Behind this final Go/No Go Systems Check there are thousands of individual measurements and sensors that all have to be within specific tolerances before the individual system managers give the “go” call.
	<video src="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wMYfWO8DYgQ"></video>
</callout>

A good set of requirements does more than ensure a quality final design.  Requirements documents are a primary form of communication for engineers.  They let you get new team members up to speed on the project, allow you to hand your design to someone else (a teammate, a judge, a future version of yourself), and they can evaluate it without asking you what the design is meant to do. 
This reading will teach you how to write clear, precise requirements that both help ensure design quality and facilitate communication within your team.
This reading covers the following sections of a formal requirements document:
1. **Operating Scenarios**: how to describe the situations your system must handle before writing any requirements
2. **Functional Requirements**: how to specify what your system must do
3. **Performance Requirements**: how to specify how well it must do it
4. **Interface Requirements**: how to specify what your subsystem needs from, or provides to, other subsystems
5. **Should Requirements**: how to capture desirable but non-mandatory goals
6. **Key Driving Requirements**: how to identify which requirements are most critical

## Operating Scenarios
Before writing a single requirement, you need to understand how your system will actually be used. That means writing down the scenarios your design has to handle, not just the easy ones, but the realistic range of situations it will encounter.
This collection of scenarios is called a <span color="red">**`Concept of Operations`**</span>, or **ConOps**. It describes what the system needs to do and under what conditions, before you commit to any design decisions. Think of it as the answer to: *What is this system's job?*
Operating scenarios come from several sources:
- **The operating environment**: the physical conditions, infrastructure, and external constraints the system will actually face. A bridge must handle traffic loads, wind, thermal expansion, and seismic activity. A satellite must survive launch vibration, vacuum, and radiation that no lab fully replicates.
- **Stakeholders**: the people who will use, operate, or be affected by your system. A medical device engineer interviews surgeons and patients; an automotive engineer talks to drivers, mechanics, and safety regulators. Their expectations and workflows define what the system must handle.
- **Known failure modes and edge cases**: past experience with similar systems, fault analysis, and reasoning about what can go wrong. Aircraft designers write scenarios for engine-out and hydraulic failure conditions not because they expect them, but because a system that only works when everything goes right is not a safe system.
<callout color="gray_bg">
	**Competition Robotics Example**
	Here is how one student team might describe the operating scenarios for a shooter subsystem for a competition robot.
	- **Close Shooting:** The shooter rapidly shoots three balls from the close zone and is ready to receive more within 1 second. There may be contact between robots from any direction; the robot shoots accurately while being pushed.
	- **Far Shooting:** The shooter rapidly shoots three balls from the far zone and is ready to receive more within 1 second. There may be contact from the side; the robot shoots accurately while being pushed sideways.
	- **Shoot While Moving:** The robot roams around the close zone collecting balls and shooting them while on the move.
	- **Shooter Jam (Edge Case):** An artifact sticks in the shooter. The shooter clears jammed artifacts automatically or with driver input.
	*Notice that the last scenario (a jam) is an *<span color="green_bg">***edge case***</span>*, not the primary use. Good operating scenarios include things that go wrong, not just the ideal path. A system that only works when everything goes right is not a reliable system.*
</callout>
<empty-block/>
Once you have your scenarios written, your requirements should follow directly from them. If you find yourself writing a requirement that doesn't trace back to any operating scenario, you should ask whether that requirement is really necessary. On the other hand, if you your requirements don’t cover all of your ConOps scenarios, you have a gap in your requirements.
<callout color="gray_bg">
	🔵 <span color="blue">**Advanced ConOps**</span>
		A basic ConOps covers the primary operating scenarios.  For a low-stakes or novice engineering project, this is often sufficient to get the project moving.  As projects increase in complexity more advanced ConOps cover more scenarios including increasingly less likely edge cases
		An advanced ConOps goes beyond describing the scenarios and also defines what success looks like for each scenario. 
		Basic Scenario
		> The shooter shoots accurately while being pushed.
		Advanced Scenario
		> The shooter achieves 28/30 accuracy from the close zone under lateral contact.
		When your ConOps includes success criteria, writing verifiable performance requirements becomes much more direct.  You are essentially formalizing what you already wrote.
</callout>

## Functional Requirements
A <span color="red">**`functional requirement`**</span> answers the question: *What must this system do?* It describes a function: an action the system must be capable of performing.
Functional requirements are written as <span color="red">**`shall`**</span> statements. The word <span color="red">**`shall`**</span> is not just a stylistic choice; it signals that this is a mandatory requirement. If the system doesn't do this, it fails.
<callout color="gray_bg">
	Here are the functional requirements from the student shooter requirements:
	1. The shooter **shall** propel a ball from the robot into the goal opening.
	2. The shooter **shall** receive a ball from the intake subsystem and hold it in a ready-to-fire position.
	3. The shooter **shall** return to a ready-to-receive state after each firing cycle without driver intervention.
</callout>
Each of these statements describes one function. **One function per statement** is the rule, not because it's a formatting preference, but because it makes requirements testable. If a requirement describes two functions, you can't tell whether a failure means the system failed the first one, the second one, or both.
Notice also that these requirements describe *what* the system does, not *how*. "The shooter shall propel a ball" says nothing about whether it uses a flywheel, a pneumatic piston, or a rubber band. That's intentional: requirements define the problem, not the solution.
<callout color="gray_bg">
	<details>
	<summary>🔵 <span color="blue">**Advanced Functional Requirements**</span></summary>
		At the basic level, a functional requirement just needs to be a clear, testable shall statement describing one function. At a more advanced level, the set of functional requirements as a whole needs to hold together: every function in the ConOps is covered, nothing is missing, nothing overlaps, and each requirement can be justified.
		**Completeness** 
			Completeness means every operating scenario in the ConOps generates at least one functional requirement. A quick way to check: go through each scenario and ask, "What does the system need to be able to *do* to handle this?" If you can't point to a shall statement that covers it, you have a gap.
		**No overlap**
			No overlap means each requirement covers a distinct function. If two requirements could both be violated by the same failure, they're probably describing the same function twice. Redundant requirements create confusion during testing: which one did the system actually fail?
		**Rationale**
			Rationale means every requirement has a documented reason for existing: what ConOps scenario or stakeholder need it traces to, and any assumptions behind it. Rationale is what separates a requirement you can defend from a requirement you just wrote down.
			<callout color="gray_bg">
				> **Functional requirement**: The shooter **shall** clear a jammed artifact and return to a ready-to-fire state.
				> **Rationale**: Derived from the Shooter Jam edge case scenario. Any jam without a recovery path results in complete scoring loss for the remainder of the match.
			</callout>
		**Validation**
			Validation is the process of checking the full set of requirements before committing to fabrication. At the advanced level, you should be able to apply each of the following 6 criteria to every requirement in your document:
			- [ ] **Correctly written**: each requirement is a single shall statement with one function or performance threshold, free of ambiguity
			- [ ] **Technically correct**: the requirement reflects an accurate understanding of the system and its operating environment
			- [ ] **Stakeholder-satisfying**: the requirement traces to a real need from someone who cares about the outcome (drive team, alliance partners, game rules)
			- [ ] **Feasible**: the requirement is actually achievable given your materials, tools, and timeline
			- [ ] **Verifiable**: you can design a test that produces a clear pass or fail result
			- [ ] **Non-redundant**: the requirement is unique and not duplicated elsewhere in the document
			A requirement that fails any of these criteria should be revised or removed before you build. Catching a bad requirement on paper is much cheaper than discovering it after fabrication.
	</details>
</callout>

## Performance Requirements
A functional requirement tells you what the system must do. A <span color="red">**`performance requirement`**</span> tells you **how well** it must do it.
Performance requirements are also written as shall statements, but they include a quantitative value—a number that defines the threshold between passing and failing.
<callout color="gray_bg">
	Here are the performance requirements from the student shooter requirements:
	1. Consistency: The shooter <span underline="true">shall</span> score a minimum of 25/30 of balls in test scenario.
	2. Throughput: The shooter **shall** complete a firing cycle in 2 seconds or less from ready-to-fire position.
	3. Reliability: The shooter **shall** operate for the full 2.5-minute match duration shooting at least 60 balls without mechanical failure.
</callout>
Performance requirements differ from functional requirements in that they can be verified with a test. "score a minimum of 25/30 of balls" — you can run that test right now. "Complete a firing cycle in 2 seconds or less" — you can time it. That testability is the point of a performance requirement.
If you find yourself writing a performance requirement that you can't imagine testing, the requirement is probably not written correctly. "The shooter shall be reliable" is not a performance requirement. It's a wish. "The shooter shall run 60 consecutive firing cycles without mechanical failure" is a testable performance requirement.
<callout color="gray_bg">
	<details>
	<summary>🔵 <span color="blue">**Advanced Performance Requirements**</span></summary>
		At the basic level, a performance requirement needs a quantitative threshold in a shall statement.  At the advanced level, each performance requirement comes with a full verification plan: a rationale tracing the performance requirements to the ConOps, a **`measure of performance`** (**`MOP`**) defining exactly how the requirement will be tested, and **`technical performance measures`**** **(**`TPM`**) establishing how the team expects the measurement to progress from the the start of the build toward the final product.
		**Rationale**
			The number in a performance requirement is only as good as the reasoning behind it. Rationale should show where the threshold came from: a scoring model, a game rule, a cycle time calculation, not just restate what the requirement says.
			<callout color="gray_bg">
				> **Requirement:** The shooter shall complete a firing cycle in 2 seconds or less.
				> **Rationale:** A full teleop cycle must complete in under 5 seconds to achieve 20 cycles per match. Transfer is estimated at 1 second, leaving 3 seconds for the shooter. This is a KDR: if cycle time exceeds 3 seconds, the match scoring model fails regardless of accuracy.
			</callout>
		**Measures of Performance (MOPs)**
			A MOP defines the exact test that will verify the requirement: what is measured, how it is measured, how many trials are required, and what counts as a pass. A performance requirement without a MOP is an intention; a requirement with a MOP is a commitment to holding the design to a specific standard of performance.
			<callout color="gray_bg">
				> **MOP:** Time from artifacts in ready position to third artifact leaving shooter, measured by video analysis over 10 consecutive trials. Pass = ≤ 2.0 seconds.
			</callout>
		**Technical Performance Measures (TPMs)**
			A TPM establishes a predicted value at the start of the project and tracks measured values at each build iteration. The purpose is not to predict perfectly: it's to surface problems early, while there's still time to respond.
			<callout color="gray_bg">
				> **TPM:** Predicted value at project start: 3 seconds. Track measured average cycle time at each build iteration.
			</callout>
	</details>
</callout>
### Measures of Performance
A performance requirement tells you what the system must achieve. A <span color="red">**`Measure of Performance`**</span> (<span color="red">**`MOP`**</span>) tells you exactly how you will verify it.
A MOP is a specific test protocol: what you measure, how you measure it, how many trials will be run, and what counts as a pass. MOPs turn requirements into test plans.
<callout color="gray_bg">
	**Requirement:** The shooter shall complete a firing cycle in 2 seconds or less.
	**MOP:** Time from ball in ready position to third ball leaving shooter, measured by video analysis over 10 consecutive trials. Pass = ≤ 2.0 seconds.
</callout>
The MOP answers questions the requirement leaves open: What exactly are we timing? How many times do we run the test? What tool do we use to measure it? Without these answers, two teammates could run the same test and get results that are impossible to compare objectively.
📌 **On sample size**
The student team chose 10 trials for this MOP. That's not arbitrary. It reflects the variability they expect in motor spinup time and the consequence of a false pass. You don't need a statistics course to think about this: ask yourself, *"If the system passes this test once, am I confident it will work in a match?"* If the answer is no, you need more trials.
<callout color="gray_bg">
	<details>
	<summary>🔵 <span color="blue">**Advanced — Tolerances and sample size justification**</span></summary>
		**Tolerances**
			A MOP threshold like "≤ 2.0 seconds" looks precise, but any physical measurement has uncertainty. Your timer has reaction error. The mechanism has cycle-to-cycle variability. If the system runs at 2.1 seconds consistently, is that a pass or a fail? The answer depends on where your uncertainty comes from.
			This is what **`tolerance`** is for. A tolerance specifies the acceptable band around a threshold, with a justification that traces to real sources of variability in the system or the measurement.
			<callout color="gray_bg">
				> **MOP tolerance:** Pass threshold = 2.0s ± 0.2s. Tolerance based on ±0.03 video frame rate, blurriness of balls exiting the shooter, and observed mechanical variability in early testing.
			</callout>
			The tolerance isn't a fudge factor. It's an engineering judgment about what variability is real and what is measurement noise. If your tolerance is so wide that the test can't distinguish a good system from a bad one, the MOP isn't doing its job.
		**Sample size**
			Sample size deserves the same rigor. The right number of trials depends on two things: how variable the system is, and how bad a false pass would be. For the reliability MOP (60 consecutive cycles without failure), the student team required 3 full runs. One successful run might be luck; three is evidence. For the jam-clearing MOP, they used only 5 trials, but justified it: jams vary by artifact orientation, so a small number of worst-case induced jams is more informative than a large number of easy ones.
	</details>
</callout>
<callout color="gray_bg">
	<details>
	<summary>🔵 <span color="blue">**Advanced — Technical Performance Measures and build tracking**</span></summary>
		A MOP tells you whether the finished system passes or fails. A <span color="red">**`Technical Performance Measure`**</span> (<span color="red">**`TPM`**</span>) tells you whether you are *on track* to pass, before the system is finished.
		A TPM is a parameter you measure at each build iteration and compare to a predicted value. The gap between predicted and actual is information: it tells you whether your design is converging on the requirement or diverging from it.
		<callout color="gray_bg">
			> **Requirement:** The shooter shall complete a firing cycle in 2 seconds or less.
			> **TPM:** Predicted value at project start: 3 seconds. Track measured average cycle time at each build iteration.
		</callout>
		The predicted value at project start is the team's best estimate before they've built anything. It may be wrong. The point is not to be right on day one; it's to have a number you can update as evidence comes in. If after three iterations the measured cycle time is plateauing at 2.4 seconds, you have a decision to make: redesign the mechanism, or revise the requirement. Both are legitimate options, but the decision should be documented, with rationale, so the team knows what they traded away and why.
	</details>
</callout>

## Interface Requirements
So far, the requirements we’ve discussed describe what one system must do on its own. But in complex projects, each subsystem has to connect to other subsystems.  Often separate individuals or teams work on two subsystems in isolation. An <span color="red">**`interface requirement`**</span> specifies what one subsystem needs from, or provides to, another subsystem at the boundary where they meet.
Interfaces come in a few types:
- **Physical**: mounting patterns, shaft alignment, envelope limits, how one part has to fit against or connect to another.
- **Electrical**: voltage, current draw, connector type, wiring.
- **Data/signal**: what information gets passed between subsystems, and how.
- **Sequencing**: what has to happen, or be true, before or after another subsystem acts.
<callout color="gray_bg">
	**Competition Robotics Example**
	The intake and shooter subsystems on a competition robot share a physical and sequencing interface:
	> The intake **shall** deliver a ball to the shooter's ready position within ±5 mm of the shooter's intake point.
	> The shooter **shall** signal ready-to-receive to the intake within 0.5 seconds of completing a firing cycle.
	Notice this is really *two* requirements pointing in opposite directions across the same boundary, one on what the intake must deliver, one on what the shooter must communicate back. A single interface almost always generates requirements on both sides.
</callout>
Interface requirements are still shall statements, and they still need to follow the same rules: one function per statement, verifiable, non-redundant. What's different is where they come from. A functional requirement about your own subsystem is yours to decide. An interface requirement describes a boundary you share with someone else's subsystem, which means you can't write it alone.
**Before you finalize an interface requirement, it should be confirmed with the team that owns the subsystem on the other side of that boundary.** If both teams independently guess what the interface should be, you'll likely end up with two subsystems that don't actually agree, and you won't find out until you try to put them together.
<callout color="gray_bg">
	<details>
	<summary>🔵 <span color="blue">**Advanced Interface Requirements**</span></summary>
		At the basic level, an interface requirement just needs to be a verifiable shall statement, confirmed with the teammate on the other side. At a more advanced level, treat the interface itself as something you're jointly designing, not just describing.
		**Negotiated, not assumed**
			Document who you coordinated with and when. If the interface changes later (a mounting pattern shifts, a signal timing changes), that's a change to a shared agreement, not just your own design, it should go through the same change control as any other requirement update, and the teammate on the other side needs to know.
		**Tolerance stack-up across the boundary**
			A physical interface requirement with a tolerance (like ±5 mm above) only works if both sides of the boundary are actually built to it. If your subsystem's manufacturing tolerance is ±3 mm and your teammate's is ±3 mm, your combined stack-up at the interface could be ±6 mm, wider than the interface requirement allows. Advanced interface requirements account for this explicitly rather than assuming nominal dimensions will line up.
		<callout color="gray_bg">
			> **Interface requirement:** The intake shall deliver a ball to the shooter's ready position within ±5 mm of the shooter's intake point.
			> **Tolerance stack-up:** Intake mounting tolerance ±2 mm, shooter mounting tolerance ±2 mm, chassis rail tolerance ±1 mm. Combined worst-case: ±5 mm. Interface requirement is at the edge of feasibility, verify early.
		</callout>
	</details>
</callout>
## Should Requirements
Not every goal is mandatory. Some capabilities would improve your system, but the design might succeed without them. For these, you use <span color="red">**`should`**</span> instead of shall.  Should statements can be either functional requirements or performance requirements and should be listed at the bottom of  those sections to show that they are less important to the design process.
<callout color="gray_bg">
	Some should statements from the student shooter requirements:
	**Functional Should Requirements**
		> The shooter **should** clear a jammed artifact and return to a ready-to-fire state.
		> The shooter **should** support independent adjustment of launch angle and/or launch speed by the control system.
	**Performance Should Requirements**
		> The shooter **should** clear a jammed artifact and return to a ready-to-fire state within 10 seconds of jam detection, with or without driver input.
		> The shooter **should** support independent adjustment of launch angle and/or launch speed by the control system, with a launch angle range of at least X degrees and a motor speed range of at least Y to Z RPM.  X, Y, and Z to be determined in initial testing.
</callout>
The student team explains their reasoning on the jam-clearing requirement: jams are a known failure mode, but if the intake and transfer geometry are well-designed, jams may be rare enough that a dedicated jam-clearing mechanism isn't worth the added complexity. If jams turn out to be frequent, they can revisit. The should keeps the requirement in the document (visible, trackable) without committing the team to building a more complex mechanism before they have evidence that it's needed.
The distinction between shall and should is a design decision, not a writing preference. Choosing the wrong one in either direction has consequences: over-using shall locks you into requirements you may not be able to meet; over-using should lets critical functions slip out of scope.
<callout color="gray_bg">
	<details>
	<summary>🔵 <span color="blue">**Advanced Should Requirements**</span></summary>
		High-level should requirements share the same features as other functional and performance requirements.  See the Advanced sections above for details.
	</details>
</callout>

## 🔵 <span color="blue">**Key Driving Requirements**</span>
<callout color="gray_bg">
	<details>
	<summary><span color="blue">**Advanced**</span></summary>
		Not all requirements carry the same risk. A <span color="red">**`Key Driving Requirement`**</span> (<span color="red">**`KDR`**</span>) is one where failing to meet it (or discovering late that it's unachievable) has an outsized impact on the project. KDRs deserve extra attention: larger sample sizes in testing, earlier verification, and explicit tracking through TPMs.
		The student team identified accuracy and reliability as KDRs for their shooter:
		<callout color="gray_bg">
			> **Accuracy:** Highest design risk. Trajectory is sensitive to flywheel speed, artifact mass variability, and launch angle. A false pass in early testing is likely. Requires large sample size and worst-case condition testing.
			> **Reliability:** Hardest to verify early. Fatigue, heat buildup, and game piece variability are difficult to replicate in short bench tests. Requires dedicated endurance testing late in the build cycle.
		</callout>
		When you identify a KDR, flag it explicitly in your requirements document and make sure it has a TPM assigned. A KDR without a tracking plan is a risk you've named but haven't managed.
	</details>
</callout>

## Putting It Together
A requirements document is not a formality. It is the record of what you decided your system needed to do, why you decided that, and how you plan to verify it. Done well, it makes every subsequent decision easier: when you're choosing between two designs, your requirements tell you which one is better. When a test fails, your MOPs tell you exactly what failed. When you're running out of time, your KDRs tell you what to protect.
