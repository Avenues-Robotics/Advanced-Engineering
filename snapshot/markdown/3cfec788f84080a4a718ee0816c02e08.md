### Overview
In Project 2, you designed the software architecture for your team's robot. In Project 4, you turn one piece of that architecture into working code on your competition robot. You will implement one subsystem as a Java class, run it from an OpMode (autonomous or teleop), debug it systematically, verify it against its requirements, and then refine it toward a requirement it doesn't yet meet.
The engineering process matters as much as the finished code. Your commit history and your engineering notebook are the record of how you got there, and they are part of what gets assessed.
<callout icon="🎛️" color="gray_bg">
	**Project 4 and Project 5**
	Many subsystems need a feedback controller to work well: an arm that holds an angle, a flywheel that holds a speed, a slide that stops at a height. Project 5 - Control Theory is where you build and tune that controller. You can work on Projects 4 and 5 in parallel, or complete them in either order.
</callout>
<callout icon="🏅" color="gray_bg">
	**For FTC Competitors**
	Your code for this project runs on your team's competition robot, so coordinate with your software lead before you start. Make sure your subsystem is one the team actually needs, and agree on where your code will live in the team's codebase.
</callout>

### Part 2: Implement and Debug
*Goal: Build working code in small, verified steps, and keep a record of how you found and fixed problems.*
1. **Subsystem class**<br>Write your subsystem class in TeamCode.  You may need to write additional classes for supporting mechanisms.
	<empty-block/>
2. **OpMode**<br>Write an OpMode (autonomous or teleop) that runs your subsystem through each of its functions or states. Use telemetry to display the subsystem's state and key sensor values while it runs.
	<empty-block/>
	**Both Subsystem class and OpMode must:**
		- [ ] Run on a robot without errors
		- [ ] Include a Java Document Comment ([Javadoc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Javadoc#Structure_of_a_Javadoc_comment)) that describes what each class and public method does.
			A class Javadoc follows this format
			```java
/**
 * Description of what the class does and what mechanism it controls.
 *
 * @author Your Name
 */
			```
			A method Javadoc follows this format
			```java
/**
 * Description of what the method does.
 *
 * @param name1 Description of parameter.
 * @param name2 Description of another parameter.
 * @return Description of return value.
 */
			```
		- [ ] Include comments that describe what each method and major section does so a reader can follow without running the code.
	<empty-block/>
3. **Commit as you go**<br>Commit each time you reach a working checkpoint, and push at the end of every session. Each commit message should say what changed and why. See <mention-page url="https://app.notion.com/p/3f6ec788f840814c880eeffd215a3bde"/> if you need a refresher.
	<empty-block/>
4. **Debugging log**<br>Document the bugs you encountered as you implemented your code on the robot. For each one, record:
	- what you observed
	- how you reproduced it reliably
	- how you isolated the cause (telemetry, commenting out code, etc.)
	- the cause and the fix
	- how you verified the fix actually resolved the original problem
	Your debugging log should be built from the engineering notebook entries you made during the debugging process and your commit messages. Cite or link the specific entries and commits.
	<empty-block/>
<callout icon="2️⃣" color="gray_bg">
	**For SCS2 (advanced option for SCS1)**

	2. **Branches**<br>Develop your subsystem on a feature branch and merge it into the team's working code, resolving any conflicts.
	3. **Hypothesis-driven debugging**<br>Document a specific hypothesis about the cause of the bug and the test that would confirm or rule it out, before you change any code.
</callout>

### Part 4: Refine
*Goal: Improve your subsystem's performance toward a requirement it doesn't yet meet, one documented change at a time.*
Debugging (Part 2) makes your code do what you wrote it to do. Refining optimizes your code’s performance. Your verification checklist from Part 3 shows you where the gap is; this part is about closing it with evidence rather than guesswork.
1. **Choose a target**<br>From your verification checklist, choose one requirement your subsystem doesn't meet, meets inconsistently, or does not meet optimally. State the requirement, its threshold, and your current measured performance. Your target must have a number: "make it work better" is not a target, "end within 3 cm of the mark in 4 of 5 runs" is.
	Some example targets might be:
	- The robot stops within 5 cm of a floor mark from full speed in 5 of 5 trials.
	- The full autonomous routine succeeds in 9 of 10 runs, with end-pose error under 2 cm.
	- The PID controller works with overshoot below 5%, settling time under 0.8 s, and steady-state error below 10 ticks.
	<callout icon="🐞" color="gray_bg">
		**Fixing bugs is not refining**
		If your work is finding and fixing errors, it belongs in your Part 2 debugging log. It counts as refining only when you are changing working code to move a measured value toward a requirement. If you are also working on Project 5, your PID tuning can serve as your Part 4 target.
	</callout>
	<empty-block/>
2. **Iterate**<br>Change one thing at a time and measure the result after each change. For each change, record:
	- the observation that motivated it and what you believe is causing it
	- what you changed
	- the measured result
	- whether the result was an improvement, no change, or a regression, and what you will try next because of it
	If you change more than one thing at once, say so: you will not be able to tell which change caused the result. Your iteration log can be built from your engineering notebook entries and your commit messages. Cite or link the specific entries and commits.
	<empty-block/>
3. **Final result**<br>Re-run the verification for your target requirement and state whether it now passes, fails, or is inconclusive, with the evidence.
	<empty-block/>
<callout icon="2️⃣" color="gray_bg">
	**For SCS2 (advanced option for SCS1)**
	1. **Hypothesis and prediction**<br>Before each change, state what you believe is causing the gap and the result you expect if you're right. After testing, compare the measured result to your prediction. A result that surprises you is evidence too, so record what it tells you.<br>Ex. "The robot stops 8 cm past the mark. Hypothesis: the motors coast after power is cut. Prediction: setting zero-power behavior to BRAKE will reduce the overshoot to under 3 cm."
	2. **Progress tracking**<br>Keep a running table (or plot) of your target measurement after every iteration, alongside the requirement's threshold. After each iteration, state whether the subsystem is closer to the threshold, further from it, or stuck.
	3. **Pivot or persist**<br>If your tracking shows you are stuck, or your evidence shows the current approach can't reach the threshold (for example, the motor is already at full power), make an explicit decision: pivot to a different approach, or continue with a written justification for why more iteration on this approach will close the gap. Either decision is acceptable. Drifting without deciding is not.
</callout>

### Rubric
#### Software Implementation
<columns>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Proficient**
		- Classes with correctly structured fields, constructors, and methods that compile without errors and behave as intended when run.
		- Code commented to describe what each class, method, and major section does, so a reader can follow how the program works without having to run it.
		- When code doesn't work, a systematic debugging process is followed: the failure reliably reproduced, the responsible section of code isolated (for example, with telemetry/print statements or a debugger), the specific cause fixed, and the fix verified to actually resolve the original failure.
		- Code committed to version control regularly with messages that describe what changed and why, rather than committed in one large, undocumented batch.
		- Code successfully deployed to the robot controller and confirmed to run as intended on the actual hardware.
	</column>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Advanced**
		In addition to Proficient…
		- Code organized and written so a teammate could read and correctly modify it without a verbal explanation, with comments added where the reasoning behind a piece of code isn't self-evident from the code itself.
		- Code structured for efficient use and performance (ex. constants stored as variables, enums over strings, moving repeated logic into utility classes or helper methods)
		- Branches used to develop a feature or fix without disrupting the team's working code, with changes merged back cleanly and simple conflicts resolved as they arise.
		<empty-block/>
	</column>
</columns>
#### Iteration
<columns>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Proficient**
		- Identifies and documents the specific observation or test result that motivated design changes, distinguishing between what was observed and what caused it.
		- Changes one thing at a time where possible, so the effect of each change can be attributed clearly; when multiple changes are made simultaneously, acknowledgment that the individual contributions cannot be separated.
		- Records the result of each change as an improvement, no change, or a regression, and uses this to decide what to change next.
		<empty-block/>
	</column>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Advanced**
		In addition to Proficient…
		- Systematic failure diagnosis: before changing anything, a hypothesis is generated, with the evidence that would prove or disprove it.
		- Each iteration designed as a test of a specific hypothesis, with the result that would confirm or refute it documented in advance, and the outcome evaluated against that prediction.
		- Progress toward requirements tracked across iterations: after each build cycle, an explicit assessment of whether the subsystem is closer to, further from, or stuck relative to the relevant MOP or functional requirement.
		- Recognition when an approach is fundamentally limited (when continued iteration is unlikely to close the gap to requirements), with an explicit decision to either pivot to a different approach or formally justify continuing.
	</column>
</columns>
#### Technical Communication
<columns>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Proficient**
		- Written explanations that connect a design decision, test result, or prediction to the underlying engineering reasoning, requirement, or data, using accurate technical vocabulary.
		- A clear, specific claim about whether a design meets a requirement, supported by evidence such as test data, CAD verification, or calculations.
		- Evidence that is relevant and sufficient to support the claim, including quantitative evidence (measurements, calculations, statistics) where available.
		- Because technical writing is a developing skill, proficient work successfully communicates the claim and evidence, but may contain minor errors in organization, clarity, or vocabulary use.
	</column>
	<column ratio="50">
		**Advanced**
		- Explanations and arguments that are logically coherent, using clear, quantitative reasoning to connect evidence to the claim rather than asserting a conclusion.
		- Quantitative reasoning used to reflect on the strength of the claim, including its limitations, sources of uncertainty, or where more evidence would be needed.
		- Writing that is concise and efficient, communicating complex engineering ideas at a level of depth and technicality appropriate to the intended audience (a teammate, a judge, a general reader).
		- Clear multi-paragraph structure that separates distinct ideas, design intent, evidence, and conclusion, rather than blending them together.
	</column>
</columns>
